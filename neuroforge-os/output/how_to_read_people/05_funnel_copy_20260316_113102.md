Here is something that tends to surprise people when I say it:

The deepest misreads in most people's lives don't happen with strangers. They happen with the people they know best.

A stranger surprises you once. A close friend, a partner, a colleague you've worked alongside for years — they surprise you in ways that feel like a betrayal of the entire relationship. *How did I not see this?* It's the question underneath so much of the pain that brings people to this kind of work.

The answer is not that you weren't paying attention. It's that you were paying attention to the wrong thing.

When we know someone well, the brain does something efficient and unhelpful: it builds a predictive model of them. Instead of continuing to observe the actual person, it starts consulting the model. It fills in gaps with assumptions. It notices things that confirm what it already believes and quietly overlooks things that don't fit.

You stop seeing who someone is. You start seeing who you decided they were — sometimes years ago.

This is called familiarity bias, and it's one of the reasons that people in long-term relationships often find themselves arguing with their model of the other person rather than the actual person. It's also why the friend who "suddenly changed" had usually been changing gradually for quite a while — and the signals were present, but they ran up against a fixed belief you held about who they were.

The corrective is not more vigilance. It is deliberate freshness — treating someone you know well with the quality of attention you'd bring to a first meeting. Not always. Periodically. When something feels slightly off and you can't name why.

One practical thing you can try this week:

Think of someone you're very close to. Write down five things you believe to be true about their current emotional state. Now ask yourself: when did I last actually check those beliefs against what they've shown me — rather than just what I've assumed? If the answer is "a while ago," that gap is worth gently closing.

Empathetic people work hard to understand the other person's perspective. When conflict arises, they examine their own contribution first. They extend good faith. Here is the question I want you to hold:

When you leave a difficult conversation feeling confused, what is the confusion *about*? If you find yourself reliably asking "what did I do wrong?" — that is one kind of confusion. It prompts you to reflect on your own behaviour and eventually produces clarity.

But if you find yourself asking "did that even happen the way I remember it?" — if your confusion is primarily about whether your perception of reality is trustworthy — that is a different kind of confusion entirely. And it is one that genuine misunderstanding almost never produces.

The direction of doubt is a signal. When it consistently points toward the validity of your own perception rather than the quality of your communication, the relationship is worth examining more carefully.
