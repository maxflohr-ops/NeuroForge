### VERDICT

### ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Ambady citation is imprecise and potentially misleading
**Location:** "The Gap Between Feeling It and Naming It," paragraph 2 — *"Research on what psychologist Nalini Ambady called thin slices of behaviour suggests that very brief exposures to social information...can produce judgements that are surprisingly predictive."*
**Problem:** Ambady's thin-slices research is real and citable, but the original studies focused heavily on predicting teacher effectiveness from brief classroom clips — not general interpersonal people-reading. Using it to support the broader claim that instinctive reads of people are "surprisingly predictive" goes further than the research's demonstrated scope. "Surprisingly predictive" is also vague in a way that invites a reader to overestimate the finding. NeuroForge's credibility depends on not stretching citations past their demonstrated range.
**Required fix:** Either specify what the Ambady research actually found and in what context (e.g., "in studies of teacher effectiveness, observers watching as little as six seconds of classroom footage predicted end-of-semester student ratings with significant accuracy"), or broaden the attribution to "research into thin-slices judgements" without naming Ambady specifically if the specific finding can't be confirmed. Do not present this as straightforwardly applicable to all interpersonal judgements without that qualification.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** "What Reading People Actually Is" section loses propulsive momentum and becomes defensive
**Location:** The "What Reading People Actually Is" section, particularly paragraphs 2–4 — *"It is, at its best, a form of deep attention...The most skilled readers of people I have ever encountered are not the most suspicious ones..."*
**Problem:** This section exists to pre-empt a concern the reader may not yet have. The chapter is building momentum — the reader is being drawn in, identifying with the framework, beginning to trust the author — and this section stops to argue against a counterargument that hasn't been raised. The tone shifts from guiding to justifying. The line "It is, at its best, a form of deep attention" is the closest the chapter comes to a motivational-poster register, and it does not match the surrounding voice. Defensiveness at this stage signals authorial insecurity rather than earned authority, and in a chapter designed to teach readers to trust their own perceptions, the author's visible need for reassurance is an ironic misalignment.
**Required fix:** Either cut this section to a single transitional paragraph (one sentence naming what this is not, one sentence naming what it is, then move directly to "What You'll Build") or restructure it so it earns its length by making a fresh observation rather than defending against objections. The content about "chronic vigilance without structure produces anxiety, not clarity" is the sharpest line and should be kept — it makes an actual claim. The rest of the section should be cut or rebuilt around that idea.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Two subheadings function as labels, not pulls
**Location:** "What Social Training Actually Teaches You" and "What Reading People Actually Is"
**Problem:** Per the manuscript checklist, subheadings should create pull through claim or curiosity — not simply label the section below. "What Social Training Actually Teaches You" is a category title. "What Reading People Actually Is" is a definition title. Neither creates any reason for the reader to continue. By contrast, "The Three Reasons People Dismiss Their Own Instincts" and "The Gap Between Feeling It and Naming It" both work — they signal a specific structure or a distinction the reader wants resolved.
**Required fix:** Rewrite both subheadings to embed a claim or a tension. For "What Social Training Actually Teaches You," the actual argument of the section is that social training teaches you to suppress your own signal in service of politeness — write a subheading that names that. For "What Reading People Actually Is," the actual content is a reframe from suspicion to attention — write a subheading that states the reframe rather than promising a definition.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 4:** The emotional intelligence trap is underwritten relative to its quality
**Location:** "Why Emotional Intelligence Without Structure Produces Frustration," paragraphs 2–3 — *"Emotional intelligence without structure also produces a particular trap: over-identification with others' emotional states..."*
**Problem:** This is the freshest, most counterintuitive idea in the chapter — the insight that high emotional intelligence can produce a specific kind of inaccuracy, not just exhaustion. This is the idea that makes a reader stop and say *that's exactly what happens to me.* Currently, it receives two paragraphs and is then closed down quickly. It deserves one more beat: either a specific, recognisable scenario (the moment where over-identification produces the wrong read), or a single sentence that sharpens the paradox before moving on. Without it, the section makes the claim and leaves, when it should make the claim, prove it briefly, and let it land.
**Required fix:** Add one concrete illustrative moment — not a case study, just a single sentence that names the specific scenario in which over-identification produces inaccuracy. Something that makes the reader recognise their own experience, the way the opening paragraphs do. Keep it to 2–3 sentences maximum.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 5:** "What You'll Build" section is preview-only and contributes no new idea
**Location:** "What You'll Build," all paragraphs
**Problem:** This section describes the structure of the book without adding any conceptual content. Every sentence is a navigation marker. As a roadmap, it is clear and well-written — but in a chapter that otherwise earns its length by making genuine claims, this section asks the reader to wait. In a first chapter, a preview section has to do double duty: orient the reader *and* make them feel the framework is going to be worth following. Currently, it orients but doesn't compel. The final paragraph ("None of these skills exist in isolation...") gestures at the system-nature of the method but does not make that feel exciting or distinct.
**Required fix:** Either fold the roadmap content into the chapter close (which already handles the bridge to Chapter 2 well) and cut this section, or add one sentence that makes the *architecture* of the framework feel like a fresh approach — something that tells the reader not just what is coming but why this sequencing is different from how people usually think about reading people.
**Severity:** Minor

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The chapter's primary issues cluster around a single structural tendency: sections that exist to pre-empt objections or preview structure rather than to advance the argument. "What Reading People Actually Is" defends before the attack; "What You'll Build" navigates without compelling. In both cases, the writing shifts from propulsive to administrative. This is a common AI generation pattern — inserting justification and roadmap sections that a human author would either cut or fold into load-bearing content.

**Producing agent:** Manuscript Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following constraint to the Manuscript Agent prompt for introductory chapters: *"Do not include sections whose primary function is to defend the book's approach against anticipated objections, or sections whose primary function is to preview upcoming chapters. If a defence or roadmap is necessary, it must earn its place by making a fresh claim — not by managing the reader's expectations. Every section must advance the argument, not protect it."* Additionally, add a checklist item: *"Does every subheading contain a claim or a tension — not a topic label or a definition prompt? If a subheading could be the title of a Wikipedia section, rewrite it."*
