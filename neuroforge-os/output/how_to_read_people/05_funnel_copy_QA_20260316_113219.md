### ISSUES FOUND

---

**Issue 1:** Self-assessment section included in deliverable
**Location:** Section 5 — "Funnel Copy Quality Check" and "Top 1% Standard — Self-Assessment"
**Problem:** This section is internal production documentation — a checklist the producing agent ran against its own output. It is not funnel copy. If this file is handed to a designer, developer, or publisher, this section will either cause confusion or, worse, be accidentally published. It also represents the producing agent marking its own work, which is precisely what this QA process exists to replace. Its presence in the deliverable suggests the producing agent does not have a clear boundary between its working notes and its output.
**Required fix:** Strip Section 5 entirely from the client-facing deliverable. If the producing agent's internal checklist is useful for the QA process, it should be submitted as a separate document, not embedded in the copy package.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Unqualified semi-scientific claim — nervous system processing
**Location:** Email 1, paragraph 6 — *"Your nervous system processes behavioural signals faster than your conscious mind can articulate them."*
**Problem:** This is a simplified, semi-scientific claim that is presented as established fact without any qualification. It gestures at dual-process theory and implicit processing research, but the phrasing implies a specific neurological mechanism that the copy does not — and cannot — support. Luna Hart's brand credibility depends on never overclaiming. This sentence sounds confident and scientific, but a reader with any background in psychology or neuroscience will notice that it is not quite accurate as stated. More importantly, it is not necessary — the surrounding sentences make the point about instinct and pattern recognition without needing a scientific-sounding mechanism to back them up.
**Required fix:** Either remove the sentence entirely (the paragraph works without it) or reframe it as an observation rather than a neurological claim — e.g., *"The instinct arrived before you had language for it"* — which preserves the meaning without implying a physiological process.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** "Familiarity bias" used as established technical term without qualification
**Location:** Email 2, paragraph 7 — *"This is called familiarity bias, and it's one of the reasons that people in long-term relationships often find themselves arguing with their model of the other person rather than the actual person."*
**Problem:** "Familiarity bias" is used here as if it is a named, peer-reviewed cognitive phenomenon with a settled definition. In reality, the term is used loosely across psychology, behavioural economics, and popular writing to mean several different things — it is not a single, established concept in the way that, say, confirmation bias is. The phenomenon being described in this email is real and well-documented (it draws on predictive coding, the related concept of the "mental model," and research on relationship perception over time), but the label "familiarity bias" is not the correct or consensus term for it. Applying a specific-sounding label to a loosely defined concept is a credibility risk — it reads as authoritative to most readers, but will read as imprecise to anyone with a psychology background, and Luna Hart's audience is likely to include exactly those people.
**Required fix:** One of two options. Option A: Remove the label entirely and describe the phenomenon directly — *"When we know someone well, the brain does something efficient and unhelpful: it builds a predictive model of them and starts consulting that model rather than the actual person."* This is already in the copy and is the stronger sentence anyway. Option B: Replace "familiarity bias" with a more accurate framing — *"a version of confirmation bias applied to people we think we know"* — which is both more accurate and more recognisable to the reader. Do not retain the sentence as currently written.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 4:** Objection handler breaks voice
**Location:** Section 2, Thank You Page — Objection Handler 1: *"$17 is small, but I'm not sure it's worth it."*
**Problem:** The response to this objection opens with: *"How to Read People won't change your life the day you buy it. It will change it the week after."* The framing "change your life" is exactly the kind of language Luna Hart's voice — and NeuroForge's brand — works against throughout this package. The book description explicitly distances itself from transformation-language (*"This is not a list of tips to memorise"*); Email 3 is built on precision and restraint; the landing page never once promises a life change. Introducing "change your life" here, even in a rhetorical setup, is a tonal break. It also leans on a cliché that the rest of the copy earns the right to avoid.
**Required fix:** Reframe the response without using "change your life" as the pivot. The underlying argument — that the value is not immediate but shows up in the next conversation — is sound and on-brand. Rework the opening to stay in Luna's register: something closer to *"It won't feel like much the day you read it. It will feel like something a week later, when you're in a conversation and you notice something you would have filed away before."* The producing agent should rewrite this handler in Luna's voice without the "change your life" construction.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 5:** One vague claim in book description
**Location:** Section 2, Book Description, paragraph 4 — *"There are also chapters on the specific patterns that intelligent, empathetic people consistently miss"*
**Problem:** The phrase "specific patterns" promises specificity that the sentence then does not deliver. The rest of the sentence names one example (the empathy-and-manipulation dynamic), but "chapters on the specific patterns" (plural) is not followed by any indication of what those other chapters or patterns are. Compared to the rest of the book description — which names baselines, attachment patterns, stress testing, narrative decoding, and warmth-versus-safety — this sentence underperforms. It is the one moment where the copy retreats to a vague promise.
**Required fix:** Either name one more specific chapter or pattern that belongs in this category, or cut "There are also chapters on the specific patterns" and fold the empathy-manipulation point directly into the preceding paragraph as the closing example. Do not leave a vague "chapters on the specific patterns" claim unsupported.
**Severity:** Minor

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The producing agent included its own internal QA checklist as Section 5 of the deliverable. This is a significant structural problem — the agent does not have a clean boundary between its working process and its output. The semi-scientific claims (nervous system processing, "familiarity bias") suggest the agent is pattern-matching to scientific-sounding language that fits the content without checking whether those specific claims are accurate or appropriately qualified. This is a credibility risk that will recur.

**Producing agent:** Funnel Agent

**Recommended prompt change (two additions):**

First, add an explicit instruction: *"Your deliverable contains only publishable copy. Do not include self-assessment notes, internal checklists, or quality-check documentation in the output file. If you need to document your own process, do so in a separate internal note that is never included in the copy package."*

Second, add a credibility constraint: *"Any claim that references psychology, neuroscience, or behavioural science must be either (a) a direct, accurate reference to a named and established concept, or (b) framed as an observation or pattern rather than a scientific mechanism. Do not use scientific-sounding terminology to add authority to a claim unless you can confirm the term is accurate and consistently defined. When in doubt, use plain language — the observation is more persuasive than the label."*
