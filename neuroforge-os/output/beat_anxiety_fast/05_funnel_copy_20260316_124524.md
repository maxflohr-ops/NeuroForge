### PREVIEW TEXT

Every time you check for reassurance, you're teaching your nervous system something. It's not what you think.

---

### EMAIL BODY

Here is something that most people find uncomfortable when they first hear it.

Every time you seek reassurance — from a friend, from Google, from your own repetitive self-talk — you get a few minutes of relief. Sometimes twenty minutes. Sometimes less. And then the anxiety returns, often at the same intensity or higher.

Most people interpret this as evidence that the reassurance wasn't convincing enough. That they need more of it, or more specific reassurance, or a more credible source. So they seek more.

The mechanism is actually running in the opposite direction.

When you seek reassurance and receive it, your nervous system records two things simultaneously. First: the situation was threatening enough to require checking. Second: you could not tolerate the uncertainty without external resolution. Both of those are threat-confirmations, not threat-removals. The reassurance ritual doesn't close the anxiety loop. It writes it deeper.

Over time, the urge to check becomes part of the anxiety itself. The anxiety produces the urge to seek reassurance, the reassurance produces temporary relief, the relief reinforces the checking, and the next ambiguous situation triggers the urge faster than the last one did. This is a loop with a very reliable internal logic — and it's running without your awareness or consent.

Reassurance-seeking takes forms most people don't immediately recognise as the same behaviour. Googling symptoms. Re-reading sent messages. Rehearsing conversations to anticipate every possible outcome. Asking someone "do you think it'll be fine?" and, when they say yes, needing to ask again an hour later. The internal version — "I'm sure it'll be fine, I'm sure it'll be fine" — is functionally identical to the external one.

None of this developed because you're doing something wrong. It developed because it worked once. The relief was real. The problem is that it has a cost that compounds over time, and that cost is a nervous system that has been trained to be less tolerant of uncertainty with every cycle.

The intervention isn't to stop seeking reassurance through willpower. It's to understand what the urge is doing and to delay it — specifically, to wait 20 minutes before acting on it, note whether the intensity changes, and begin building evidence that the urge can be tolerated without the checking behaviour.

What most people discover is that it can. And that evidence — accumulated across repeated experiences — is what actually begins to change the pattern.

I wrote an entire chapter on this in *Beat Anxiety Fast* — including the specific forms reassurance-seeking takes that are easy to miss, and a graduated way of beginning to interrupt the loop. If this email landed for you, that chapter is probably worth your time.
