**Issue 1:** Unverified neurological claim — the 10pm–midnight anxiety spike
**Location:** Landing page bullet 5 ("Use the pre-sleep version of the protocol for nighttime anxiety — because the 10pm–midnight window has a specific neurological explanation"); Email 2 testimonial placeholder example ("Dr. Vale explained why anxiety spikes between 10 and midnight — neurologically, not just 'because it's quiet'"); Email 4 chapter list ("The neurological explanation for why anxiety intensifies between 10pm and midnight — Chapter 6"); Google Ad 3 Description 1 ("The 10pm–midnight anxiety spike isn't random. Understanding why it happens is most of the intervention.")
**Problem:** This claim appears five times across the funnel and is stated as settled neurological fact — with a specific, narrow time window (10pm–midnight) that implies precision the research does not straightforwardly support. Cortisol rhythm, circadian variation in autonomic tone, and reduced distraction-based suppression at night are all real mechanisms. But "there is a specific neurological explanation for why anxiety spikes between 10 and midnight" is not a claim that can be sourced to a single clean finding. If a reader or journalist asks for the citation, the answer needs to exist. More importantly: if the book's Chapter 6 promises this explanation and then delivers something more qualified, the funnel oversells the chapter. This is a credibility risk that compounds across every repetition.
**Required fix:** Research Agent must identify the specific mechanism(s) Nova Vale intends to cite and confirm they are accurately characterised. If the claim can be sourced, add appropriate framing: "a neurological explanation" rather than "the neurological explanation," and ensure the chapter delivers exactly what the funnel promises. If the mechanism is a convergence of several factors rather than one clean finding, the copy should reflect that — e.g., "a set of intersecting neurological factors that explain why anxiety tends to spike in the late evening" — rather than implying a single discrete mechanism. Every instance of this claim must be updated consistently.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 2:** Amygdala/prefrontal cortex characterisation overstates consensus
**Location:** Email 2, paragraphs 4–6: "Your amygdala — the brain's threat-detection centre — has already acted... The amygdala doesn't process language the way your prefrontal cortex does. Verbal reassurance is a prefrontal-level intervention. You're applying it to a subcortical-level activation."
**Problem:** The characterisation of amygdala function here — and specifically the claim that the amygdala "doesn't process language" — is a simplified model that some researchers (notably Joseph LeDoux, whose work underpins most popular amygdala narratives) have since publicly revised. LeDoux has explicitly noted that the popular "amygdala hijack" framework overstates the amygdala's role as a discrete fear centre and misrepresents how threat processing works. Nova Vale's copy isn't wrong in its practical direction — body-first intervention is well-supported — but the specific neuroscientific framing ("subcortical event," "amygdala doesn't process language") borrows from a model that is contested in the current literature. If anyone with neuroscience training reads this email, the credibility of the entire funnel is at risk. NeuroForge's brand is built on accuracy. This is the kind of claim that, if challenged, has no clean defence.
**Required fix:** The practical point — that verbal reassurance fails during an anxiety spike and body-first intervention is more effective — is defensible and should be kept. The framing needs to be updated to reflect current understanding: describe the process as one in which physiological activation precedes and partially overrides cognitive evaluation, without attributing it to a simple amygdala/prefrontal binary. The mechanism can be explained accurately without the contested neuroscience shorthand. Suggested framing direction: "By the time logical thought arrives, your nervous system has already acted — and it does not update easily in response to verbal instruction while the physiological cascade is running." This preserves the point without the contested model. Research Agent should verify the current consensus framing before revision.
**Severity:** Major

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** Two of the three substantive issues (Issues 1 and 2) are the same class of failure: specific neuroscientific claims stated with more certainty than the current evidence cleanly supports. Both involve mechanisms that are directionally accurate but draw on models that are either contested (the amygdala/prefrontal binary) or imprecisely characterised (the 10pm–midnight window). The copy's overall precision creates a context where these claims are more exposed, not less — because readers who are drawn to mechanism-level explanation are also the readers most likely to notice when a mechanism is overstated.

**Producing agent:** Funnel Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following instruction to the Funnel Agent prompt under the Credibility section: *"When including neuroscientific claims (amygdala function, cortisol timing, nervous system mechanisms), do not present simplified or popular-science models as settled fact. If a mechanism is contested or represents a convergence of factors rather than a single finding, the copy must reflect that. Prefer accurate-and-hedged over confident-and-contestable. Flag any neuroscientific claim that cannot be directly sourced to a specific, current research consensus for Research Agent verification before the copy package is finalised."*

Additionally: a pre-publication step should be added to the funnel production workflow in which any claim of the form "there is a [neurological/biological/psychological] explanation for [specific phenomenon]" is reviewed by Research Agent against current literature before the copy is approved. This is not a rewrite requirement — it is a fact-check gate on a specific claim type that recurs in Nova Vale content.
