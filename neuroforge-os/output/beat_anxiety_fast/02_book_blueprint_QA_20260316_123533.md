**Issue 2:** Walker (2017) caveat is insufficient for the credibility risk it is meant to address
**Location:** Chapter 6, source note for Walker — "Walker's book has faced some methodological criticism regarding specific statistics cited; use for the general relationship between sleep and emotional regulation but do not rely on specific figures without independent source verification."
**Problem:** Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep* has been the subject of substantial documented criticism — including from sleep researchers — regarding systematic misrepresentation of research findings, not merely uncertain statistics. The instruction as written suggests the risk is confined to numerical figures and can be resolved by checking those figures. It does not convey that the general-relationship claim itself (sleep deprivation → elevated amygdala reactivity) requires independent primary source verification, not just corroboration. If the Manuscript Agent reads this caveat as "verify the numbers, the relationship is fine," they may cite Walker for the mechanistic claim with thin independent support. The NeuroForge credibility standard requires that any source with documented methodological controversy be either independently verified against primary research before use or replaced with a primary source.
**Required fix:** Replace the Walker caveat with a stronger instruction: "Walker (2017) has faced documented methodological criticism from researchers in the field regarding systematic misrepresentation of findings — this is not a minor citation-checking issue. Do not use Walker as a primary source for any mechanistic claim. For the amygdala reactivity / sleep deprivation relationship, verify against primary sources — start with Yoo et al. (2007), 'The Human Emotional Brain Without Sleep,' Nature Neuroscience — and cite those directly. Walker may be referenced as a popular science framing text only if a primary source is cited alongside it."
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 3:** Polyvagal theory instruction does not adequately guide the Manuscript Agent on hedging clinical claims
**Location:** Chapter 3, source note for Porges (2011) — "note that the clinical applications of polyvagal theory beyond its core physiological claims remain an area of active debate, and the Manuscript Agent should not overstate the clinical certainty of derived interventions."
**Problem:** Polyvagal theory as applied to therapeutic intervention (breath work, grounding, trauma treatment) is not merely "an area of active debate" — specific claims derived from it are disputed at the mechanistic level by autonomic neuroscientists, and some popularised applications significantly outrun the evidence base. The phrase "should not overstate clinical certainty" is the right principle but gives the Manuscript Agent no concrete guidance on what counts as overstating. In a chapter that introduces the vagus nerve as "the primary pathway of parasympathetic regulation" and frames breath tools around this model, the agent needs to know exactly where to draw the line between established physiology and extrapolated clinical claim.
**Required fix:** Replace the current instruction with the following: "Polyvagal theory is cited here for its core claim regarding the vagus nerve's role in parasympathetic tone — this physiological relationship is well-supported. Do not extend the polyvagal framework to clinical claims about therapeutic interventions (e.g., 'polyvagal-informed therapy,' 'ventral vagal states,' or trauma-treatment applications). These applications are contested. The Manuscript Agent should hedge all tool-level claims with language such as 'acts via the vagus nerve's parasympathetic pathway — one well-supported mechanism among several' rather than presenting the polyvagal model as the settled explanation for breath work's effectiveness. Do not use Porges as a citation for clinical outcomes — only for the physiological model."
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 4:** Potential voice breach in Chapter 11's Recovery Day Protocol
**Location:** Chapter 11, Recovery Day Protocol, item (3) — "A single written note at the end of the day: 'The crash is the recovery. I am not failing.'"
**Problem:** The blueprint attempts to pre-empt the obvious objection by noting "this is not affirmation-speak — it is a factual statement about physiology." That framing is defensible, and the intent is sound. However, "I am not failing" crosses from factual statement into the category of self-reassurance — which is explicitly identified in Chapter 7 as an anxiety-maintenance behaviour. A reader who is attentive to the book's own logic will notice the contradiction. More importantly, the sentence "I am not failing" engages the evaluative frame (failing/not failing) rather than the physiological frame the book establishes. Dr. Nova Vale's voice specifically avoids generic reassurance and motivational language; this sentence, however brief, risks sounding like exactly that.
**Required fix:** Revise the written note instruction so that it is unambiguously physiological and non-evaluative. For example: "Write one sentence that anchors the reader in mechanism rather than evaluation — something like: 'This is cortisol decline. It will be different tomorrow.' The sentence should describe what is happening physiologically, not assess whether the person is coping correctly." The Manuscript Agent should be instructed not to use any version of the "failing/not failing" frame in this exercise.
**Severity:** Minor — but flagged because voice consistency is a brand-level concern, and the contradiction with Chapter 7's core argument could undermine the reader's trust in the book's internal logic.

### PASSED ELEMENTS

1. **The transformation arc is behavioural, specific, and testable at every point.** The "Before" and "After" states are written in concrete, observable terms — "the relief lasts about twenty minutes," "their world quietly getting smaller" — rather than the emotional-vague language that disqualifies most self-help transformation arcs. Each "After" state corresponds precisely to a chapter's deliverable. This is the clearest transformation arc seen in this content type.

2. **The source note protocol is an exceptional credibility safeguard.** Rather than providing inline citations that the Manuscript Agent might reproduce without checking, the blueprint uses a "Source note: [citation] — Manuscript Agent must verify before citing" pattern consistently throughout. This is the correct approach to citation risk management and reflects genuine awareness of how fabricated or misattributed citations enter published content. The Walker and polyvagal caveats, though needing strengthening (flagged above), demonstrate the same instinct.

3. **Chapter 5's calming-down/resetting distinction is a strong original conceptual move.** This distinction is clinically real, practically important, and almost entirely absent from popular anxiety literature. It is framed precisely, given a mechanistic explanation, and tied to a behavioural protocol that delivers the difference rather than just naming it. This is the kind of chapter that produces the recognition moment Vale's voice profile identifies as the engine of the book's momentum.

4. **Chapter 11 (The Day After) earns its place in the sequence.** The blueprint correctly identifies this as underserved territory. The chapter's core move — naming the post-episode crash as physiologically explicable rather than pathological, and naming the specific cognitive state it creates (disproportionate negative thinking, shame spiral, reduced self-regulatory capacity) — is both accurate and precisely the kind of relief-producing recognition that distinguishes this book from the competition. The transition from Chapter 10 into 11 ("a less visible problem... the day after") is one of the stronger transition writes in the blueprint.

5. **The workbook/exercise map (Section 9) is well-constructed and production-ready.** Each exercise produces a named, specific deliverable that feeds forward into the Personal Protocol Builder (Chapter 12). The architecture is cumulative — the reader is building something across the book, not completing isolated exercises — and the map makes this visible. This is exactly the structural logic that makes a protocol-focused self-help book usable rather than merely readable.

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** No red-flag failures. The recurring pattern is citation caveat under-specification — the blueprint correctly identifies credibility risks but calibrates the Manuscript Agent's instructions too lightly in two instances (Walker, polyvagal theory). The agent producing the blueprint understands the principle of citation verification but appears to default to a "note the concern briefly and move on" approach when the risk is significant enough to require more directive instruction.

**Producing agent:** Book Architect Agent (blueprint)

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following instruction to the Book Architect Agent's source note guidance: *"When flagging a source with known methodological controversy or contested clinical application, the caveat must name the specific nature of the risk (not just 'some criticism exists'), specify the scope of what should not be relied upon, and provide the Manuscript Agent with an alternative verification route. A caveat that does not redirect is not a safeguard — it is a transfer of risk."*

This would prevent the Walker and polyvagal issues from recurring and would strengthen the blueprint's downstream credibility protection across all future health and psychology titles.
