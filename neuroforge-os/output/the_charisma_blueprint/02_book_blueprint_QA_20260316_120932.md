**Issue 1:** Citation requiring verification before Manuscript Agent proceeds
**Location:** Chapter 5, Key Points — *"Bodie et al., 2012, on active listening and relational outcomes"*
**Problem:** The instruction to the Manuscript Agent reads "must locate and cite specifically" — which implies the blueprint author is not certain this citation is accurate. This is categorically different from all other source notes in this document, where specific papers are named with confidence. If this citation is speculative or a best-guess, and the Manuscript Agent cannot locate it, there is a meaningful risk of a fabricated or misattributed citation appearing in the manuscript. Bodie has published extensively on listening, and a 2012 paper is plausible, but "must locate" is not the same as "this is the paper." Separately, Chapter 11 references "Reis & Shaver, 1988" again and adds *"also see research on the 'remembering effect' in interpersonal perception — Manuscript Agent must locate and cite specifically"* — the second part of this instruction again signals uncertain sourcing.
**Required fix:** Before this blueprint is passed to the Manuscript Agent, the supervising editor or researcher must confirm: (a) that Bodie et al., 2012 exists and contains the claimed finding; (b) that an actual citation exists for "the remembering effect in interpersonal perception" or that this framing is removed from the blueprint instructions entirely. If no citation for the remembering effect can be confirmed, the blueprint should be amended to instruct the Manuscript Agent to rely solely on the Reis & Shaver, 1988 responsiveness framework for that point, which is a confirmed citation. The blueprint should not pass to the Manuscript Agent with "must locate" instructions on core empirical claims.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 2:** "Self-silencing" citation instruction is ambiguous
**Location:** Chapter 8, Key Points — *"Jack, 1991, on self-silencing"*
**Problem:** Dana Crowley Jack's 1991 work on self-silencing (*Silencing the Self*) is a real and credible source. However, the original research was conducted specifically with women experiencing depression — it is a clinically situated framework. The blueprint applies it to general self-editing in social contexts across all readers. This is not necessarily wrong, but it is a framing leap that needs to be handled carefully in the manuscript to avoid either (a) misrepresenting the source's scope or (b) inadvertently suggesting the reader may be depressed because they self-edit. The second instruction — *"also see research on impression management and authenticity costs — Manuscript Agent must locate and cite specifically"* — is again a "must locate" instruction without a named source, which repeats the Issue 1 pattern.
**Required fix:** Two distinct actions required. First: amend the blueprint to include a note to the Manuscript Agent that Jack 1991 must be framed carefully — the concept of self-silencing can be drawn on but the clinical and demographic specificity of the original research must not be obscured. Second: either supply a specific citation for impression management and authenticity costs (Leary, 2019, on impression management, or Kernis & Goldman, 2006, on authenticity costs, are likely candidates depending on the exact finding needed) or remove the "must locate" instruction and name the concept without requiring a specific citation for that point. Do not pass vague sourcing instructions to the Manuscript Agent.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 3:** Chapter 3 — potential scope overreach requiring boundary clarification
**Location:** Chapter 3, Key Points — polyvagal theory introduction; also the disclaimer note in Section 7 (Front Matter Plan)
**Problem:** The blueprint correctly includes a disclaimer noting that the book is not a substitute for therapy. However, the scope of Chapter 3 as written is broad enough that the Manuscript Agent may produce content that crosses from education into implied clinical application. Specifically: the instruction to introduce three states of the nervous system (ventral vagal, sympathetic, dorsal vagal) and connect them to the reader's lived social experience, combined with the physiological regulation sequence, is a legitimate self-help framework — Porges' work is well-established and widely applied in non-clinical contexts. The risk arises if the Manuscript Agent, following the blueprint's language closely, produces content that implies the reader can self-treat anxiety disorders, social anxiety disorder, or trauma responses using these tools. The blueprint's phrase "a physiological problem with physiological solutions" is strong and accurate, but in manuscript form it could easily tip into a medical claim if the boundary is not explicit in the blueprint itself, not just in the front matter disclaimer.
**Required fix:** Add a specific instruction to the Chapter 3 brief for the Manuscript Agent: *"Do not frame the regulation sequence as a treatment for clinical anxiety disorders or trauma. The tools in this chapter are for social regulation in non-clinical situations. Readers experiencing significant anxiety or trauma symptoms are referred to professional support. This note should be woven naturally into the chapter, not bolted on as a disclaimer paragraph."* The front matter disclaimer is not sufficient protection — the Manuscript Agent needs explicit scope instruction in the chapter itself.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 4:** Chapter 13 — identity-level change sources need hierarchy clarification
**Location:** Chapter 13, Key Points — *"see Deci & Ryan, 1985, self-determination theory; also Fogg, 2019, on identity and behaviour change; James Clear's framing in Atomic Habits, 2018, is accessible but Manuscript Agent should prioritise academic sources where possible"*
**Problem:** This source note conflates three very different types of sources: self-determination theory (academic, primary), BJ Fogg's *Tiny Habits* (2019 — practitioner-authored, not primarily an identity-change framework), and James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (popular nonfiction). The specific instruction to the Manuscript Agent — "prioritise academic sources where possible" — is ambiguous on whether popular nonfiction can be cited at all. More importantly, Fogg's framework is not primarily about identity-level change; his contribution is about behaviour design and tiny habits. Attributing identity-level change to Fogg may misrepresent the source. Clear's identity framing is well-known ("you don't rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems" and the "identity-based habits" concept) but it is itself a popularisation of ideas with roots in other researchers' work. If the Manuscript Agent cites Clear as a source for identity-level change, this is accurate in a popular nonfiction sense but not credible as a primary authority.
**Required fix:** Amend the source note to specify: (a) Deci & Ryan, 1985, for self-determination theory; (b) for identity and behaviour change specifically, point the Manuscript Agent to Verplanken & Orbell, 2003, on habit and identity, or Oyserman, 2015, on identity-based motivation — both are more directly relevant than Fogg; (c) James Clear may be referenced as a "see also" in a footnote or acknowledgement context, not as a primary citation for a theoretical claim; (d) remove Fogg from the identity-change source note unless the specific finding being cited is about behaviour design rather than identity. This is a credibility issue — the book's positioning as clinically grounded requires that its theoretical citations are accurate.
**Severity:** Minor (does not affect the blueprint's structure, but will cause a credibility problem in the manuscript if not corrected before handoff)

**Issue 5:** The chapter sequence has an arguable logic gap between Chapters 10 and 11
**Location:** Blueprint Section 6, transition from Chapter 10 to Chapter 11
**Problem:** The transition from Chapter 10 ("Visible Without Overexposed") states: *"The reader has now developed the full skill set: regulated presence, listening depth, invitational questions, group navigation, non-verbal groundedness, and calibrated vulnerability. The final challenge is the one that undoes this work most reliably — what happens when something goes wrong."* But Chapter 11 is not about things going wrong — it is about the memory effect and being unforgettable. Chapter 12 is about recovery from social misfires. The transition from Chapter 10 sets up a Chapter 12 that arrives a chapter late. Chapter 11 is correctly placed thematically — it is a capstone skill that draws on everything before it — but the transition language does not prepare the reader for it and instead promises something Chapter 11 does not deliver. This creates a sequencing confusion that the Manuscript Agent will inherit and that the human editor will need to untangle.
**Required fix:** Rewrite the Chapter 10 transition to do two jobs: close Chapter 10's disclosure calibration arc and open naturally into the memory effect. A corrected transition might read: *"The reader can now enter a conversation regulated, listen below the surface, ask questions that open doors, navigate a group, occupy their own space, and offer a genuine piece of themselves without overshooting. The question that remains — the one this book has been quietly building toward — is: what does all of this add up to for the person on the other side? What is the actual experience of someone who has spent time with you?"* This transitions into Chapter 11's core argument (feeling seen creates memorable interaction) without the mismatch. The Chapter 11-to-12 transition can then handle the "when it goes wrong" setup.
**Severity:** Minor

## PASSED ELEMENTS

1. **The transformation arc is genuinely behavioural and specific.** The "Before" and "After" states are not emotional abstractions ("feels more confident") but named, precise behavioural shifts — "asks questions that invite people to go deeper rather than questions that request information" and "can feel the difference in how a conversation moves when they do this." This is exactly the standard required and is among the strongest transformation arcs in the current blueprint library.

2. **Credibility discipline on contested findings is exemplary.** The explicit instruction to flag power posing (Cuddy et al., 2010) as replication-failed, and to frame mirror neuron claims as debated rather than settled, is the correct handling and sets a model for how this blueprint should be followed throughout the manuscript. The therapy disclaimer in the front matter is well-placed and appropriately scoped.

3. **The competitive differentiation note is honest and specific.** Rather than vaguely claiming to be "different from other books on the topic," the differentiation note names the three strongest competitors, accurately characterises what they offer, and identifies precisely what *The Charisma Blueprint* brings that none of them do. The description of *The Charisma Myth* as "primarily a performance framework" that "teaches you to produce states and signals" versus this book's starting point of "why the capacity for presence is currently blocked" is a genuine and defensible differentiation, not marketing language.

4. **The intro approach in Section 7 is correct for the brand and the audience.** Opening on the specific exhausted flatness of being pleasant for two hours while feeling invisible — "not sad, exactly. Just flat. And slightly ashamed of the flatness" — names the reader's private experience with the precision that creates the immediate sense of being understood. This is the opening gesture that earns the credibility needed for everything that follows.

5. **The exercise map in Section 9 is the structural backbone of the book.** Each exercise produces a specific, named output — not a reflection, not a journalling prompt, but a concrete artefact (a profile, a protocol, a statement, a written inventory) that the reader can return to. The cumulative arc from The Gap Inventory (Chapter 1) to The Social Identity Statement (Chapter 13) is coherent and builds toward a final product the reader owns. This is well above the standard for a book blueprint at this stage.

## PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The recurring issue in this blueprint is ambiguous sourcing instructions — specifically, the "must locate and cite specifically" instruction attached to claims where a specific source has not been identified. This appeared in Chapter 5 (Bodie et al., and "remembering effect"), Chapter 8 (impression management and authenticity costs), and as a milder version in Chapter 13 (Fogg). In every case, the effect is the same: the blueprint passes an unresolved credibility problem downstream to the Manuscript Agent, which is the agent least equipped to resolve it and most likely to fabricate a plausible-sounding citation rather than flag the gap.

**Which agent produced it:** Book Architect Agent.

**One change to prevent recurrence:** Add the following rule to the Book Architect Agent's source-handling instruction: *"When including a source note for the Manuscript Agent, you must either (a) name a specific, verified paper with author, year, and a brief description of its actual finding, or (b) write the point without a source note and flag it as 'unverified claim — requires source or removal.' You may not write 'must locate and cite specifically' for any claim where you do not already have a specific, plausible citation. If you are uncertain whether a source exists, write 'Source uncertain — Manuscript Agent should verify or remove this claim before drafting.' The human editor will resolve it. Do not pass sourcing uncertainty to the Manuscript Agent disguised as an instruction."*
