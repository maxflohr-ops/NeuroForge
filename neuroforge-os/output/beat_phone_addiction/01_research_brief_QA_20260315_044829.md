### ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Unverified statistic — phone touches per day
**Location:** Section 7, Hook 4: *"The average smartphone user touches their phone over 2,600 times per day."*
**Problem:** This is a highly specific figure that circulates widely online but traces back to a single 2016 study by Dscout, which has not been independently replicated at scale and is frequently misquoted. If this figure is fabricated or the sourcing is inaccurate, it becomes a Red Flag. It does not yet meet the automatic-fail threshold because it is a real, circulating claim — but it is close. Any downstream content that cites this as fact without verified sourcing fails immediately.
**Required fix:** Verify the original Dscout source. If confirmed, note the source and year in the brief. If unverifiable, replace with a behavioural description that does not depend on a specific number, or reframe as approximate: "Studies have estimated smartphone users touch their devices thousands of times daily — most of those touches are reflexive, not intentional." Do not carry the 2,600 figure into any published content without a confirmed citation.
**Severity:** Critical

---

**Issue 2:** Unverified causal claim about boredom and phone use
**Location:** Section 7, Hook 11: *"The research says it is the other way around — they feel bored because they check their phone."*
**Problem:** This is stated as a research finding ("the research says") but no source is named or indicated anywhere in the brief. The causal direction claim — that phone use causes boredom rather than responds to it — is a meaningful and counterintuitive assertion. If this is not traceable to a specific published study, this phrasing is a fabricated citation in soft form. It does not name a researcher, but it invokes "research" as authority for a specific causal claim. That is enough to create credibility risk.
**Required fix:** Either identify and log the specific study this references in the brief notes, or remove "the research says" and reframe as a testable hypothesis: "Most people think they check their phone because they are bored. But try the reverse: notice whether the urge to check comes before or after the bored feeling." This keeps the counterintuitive angle without making an unverified empirical claim.
**Severity:** Critical

---

**Issue 3:** Unverified claim about the designer of pull-to-refresh
**Location:** Section 7, Hook 5: *"Pull-to-refresh was deliberately modelled on the slot machine mechanic. The designer who built it no longer uses it."*
**Problem:** The first sentence is accurate and well-documented — Aza Raskin has publicly discussed infinite scroll on this basis, and the slot machine parallel is a legitimate and sourced claim in the tech ethics literature. However, the specific attribution here appears to conflate two separate claims: the slot machine mechanic is more commonly associated with variable reward systems generally and with Aza Raskin's discussion of infinite scroll specifically, not pull-to-refresh. Pull-to-refresh is attributed to Loren Brichter. The "designer who built it no longer uses it" claim is in circulation but requires a verifiable source. If the designer reference is incorrect or the attribution is muddled, this damages credibility in any piece that uses it.
**Required fix:** Clarify which specific designer and which specific feature are being referenced. If using Aza Raskin on infinite scroll, update the hook accordingly. If using Loren Brichter on pull-to-refresh, verify that he has publicly made the "no longer uses it" statement. Log the correct attribution in the brief before this hook is used in downstream content.
**Severity:** Critical

**Issue 4:** Two chapter angles overlap in scope
**Location:** Section 5, Angle 10 (*The phone-free block*) and Angle 4 (*Environmental design over willpower*)
**Problem:** Both angles address the same core prescription: create structural barriers to phone access during work time. Angle 4 covers the philosophy and architecture of friction design. Angle 10 covers the phone-free time block. In a 16-angle list, overlap is tolerable as differentiation. In a book blueprint derived from this brief, these two would risk producing redundant chapters unless the distinction is sharpened now.
**Required fix:** Clarify the distinction in the brief. Angle 4 should be scoped explicitly to *environment design* (physical placement, app removal, notification architecture). Angle 10 should be scoped explicitly to *time architecture* (how to design, communicate, and protect a phone-free block). Add one sentence to each angle's "why it matters" field that makes the distinction explicit.
**Severity:** Minor

**Issue 5:** One objection counter is abstract and does not land at the right level
**Location:** Section 6, Objection 7: *"Most social media connection is passive and asymmetric — the cost of reducing it on relationships is usually far lower than it feels before you test it."*
**Problem:** This counter is accurate but thin. It uses a conceptual frame ("passive and asymmetric") without grounding it in a specific behaviour or test. The objection — "I will miss things and feel less connected" — is emotionally loaded, and Kai Ren's voice would typically meet that with something concrete and testable, not a generalisation about relationship cost. In the current form, this counter could appear in any self-improvement brief on any topic. It does not sound like Kai Ren.
**Required fix:** Ground the counter in a specific test or observation. Example direction: *"Name the last three meaningful conversations your social media feed produced. If you cannot, your sense of connection is coming from presence in the feed, not from actual exchange — and those are not the same thing."* The human editor should rewrite in Kai Ren's voice, but the current version should not be used as-is.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 6:** Hook 12 uses an uncharacteristic qualifier
**Location:** Section 7, Hook 12: *"Most people do not know that the grayscale trick actually works — not because colour is addictive, but because it removes the visual reward cue that triggers the reach in the first place."*
**Problem:** The phrase "actually works" is slightly below Kai Ren's register. It carries a mild breathlessness that sits closer to lifestyle blog copy than to Kai Ren's clipped, declarative style. It is a minor voice drift but notable precisely because the rest of the brief holds the voice so consistently. The explanation that follows is strong — the hook just needs a cleaner entry.
**Required fix:** Remove "actually works" and restructure the opening. Direction: *"Grayscale mode is not a gimmick. It works because it removes the visual reward cue that triggers the reach — not because colour is addictive."* Adjust to Kai Ren's voice as appropriate.
**Severity:** Minor

**Issue 7:** Self-assessment checklist in Section 10 is not a reliable QA mechanism
**Location:** Section 10, Research Brief Quality Check
**Problem:** The checklist is fully self-assessed by the producing agent and includes a tick against "No fake citations or fabricated statistics included" — while three claims in Section 7 carry unverified attribution risk (Issues 1–3 above). This is not a criticism of intent; it reflects the structural limitation of self-assessment on factual claims. The checklist creates a false confidence signal if a human editor treats a ticked checklist as evidence of verified accuracy.
**Required fix:** Add a note to the checklist template that specific statistics, named studies, and attribution claims require external verification before the brief is passed. The tick for "No fake citations" should be conditional on that verification, not assumed from the absence of intent to fabricate. This is a process note, not a revision to the brief content itself.
**Severity:** Major

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The producing agent correctly avoided motivational filler, held Kai Ren's voice, and produced a well-structured brief. The recurring failure is in the handling of specific empirical claims. Three hooks in Section 7 used specific statistics or attributions that carry unverified sourcing risk. The agent's self-assessment checklist marked these as clean, which suggests the agent is checking for the *intent* to fabricate rather than the *verifiability* of each specific claim.

**Producing agent:** Research Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following instruction to the Research Agent prompt, specifically in the hooks section:

*"For any hook that includes a specific statistic, a named study, a causal claim attributed to research, or a named individual's behaviour or statement: flag it inline with [VERIFY] before the claim. Do not tick the 'No fake citations' box in the quality check until each [VERIFY] flag has been resolved with a source. If you cannot identify a source, reframe the claim as observational or hypothetical rather than empirical."*

This single change would have caught Issues 1, 2, and 3 before QA and would reduce the credibility risk in every subsequent brief produced from statistics-heavy topics.
