**Issue 2:** Unattributed statistic presented as established research — Script 2
**Location:** "After a phone interruption — even one you initiated — it takes an average of 23 minutes to return to full task engagement."
**Problem:** This figure is widely repeated but originates from a single 2008 study (Gloria Mark, UC Irvine) that is frequently misrepresented in popular media. The original research measured recovery time from workplace interruptions, not specifically phone interruptions. Presenting it as unqualified fact without attribution creates a credibility risk — if a viewer checks the claim, the mismatch between the original context and this application is apparent. This sits at the edge of the Fabricated Citation red flag: the statistic exists but is being applied beyond its verified scope with more certainty than the evidence supports.
**Required fix:** Add attribution in the script as spoken — "research from UC Irvine puts recovery time at around 23 minutes" — or soften to "research suggests recovery time averages more than 20 minutes." Do not remove the point; it is valid directionally. Remove the false precision.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 3:** Unnamed source for pull-to-refresh designer claim — Script 4
**Location:** "The designer who built pull-to-refresh — the gesture that mimics a slot machine lever — later said publicly that he no longer uses it himself."
**Problem:** This is a verifiable claim (Loren Brichter, designer of Tweetie, has made this statement publicly) but is presented without naming him. As written, it reads as an unverifiable anecdote. If a careful reader cannot verify it, they may treat the entire script's credibility as suspect. Additionally, presenting an unnamed "designer" is weaker rhetorically than naming the person — the name adds weight.
**Required fix:** Name Loren Brichter. "Loren Brichter, the designer who built pull-to-refresh, later said publicly that he no longer uses it himself." This costs nothing and removes the credibility gap.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 4:** "10 to 14 days" dopamine baseline timeline stated as a known, sourced fact — Script 7
**Location:** "Expect 10 to 14 days before the baseline shifts. Work through it, not around it."
**Problem:** This figure is presented as a specific, reliable timeline with no source or qualification. The neuroscience of dopamine recalibration does not support a precise, universal figure of this kind — individual variation is significant, and the research on this specific mechanism in the context of phone use is not mature enough to support a confident number. Stating "10 to 14 days" as fact invites a reader to test it, find their experience differs, and lose trust in the content.
**Required fix:** Soften to: "For most people, the discomfort window is one to two weeks. It is not permanent." This preserves the useful guidance while removing false precision.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 5:** "80%" figure in Script 16 hook is unanchored
**Location:** "There is a one-time, 20-minute audit that eliminates the triggers behind 80% of your compulsive phone checks."
**Problem:** The 80% figure appears in the hook — the most visible, highest-stakes line in the script — with no basis given anywhere in the script body. It is not attributed, not qualified, and not explained. It reads as a persuasion number, not a finding. This is the kind of claim that erodes the brand's credibility premium.
**Required fix:** Either remove the percentage entirely ("eliminates the triggers behind most of your compulsive phone checks") or replace the hook with a specific, defensible claim ("Most compulsive phone pickups start with a notification. Here is how to remove almost all of them"). Do not invent a sourced justification — the fix is removal of the unearned precision.
**Severity:** Major

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** Two categories appeared across this batch.

First, **false precision in statistics and timelines** — figures presented with authority that the underlying evidence does not support (23 minutes, 10–14 days, 80%, 90 seconds). The producing agent is generating credible-sounding numbers without flagging that they require verification or qualification. This is a systematic habit, not a one-off error.

Second, **incomplete submission** — the batch was submitted with three scripts missing or cut off. This suggests the producing agent either hit a generation limit and submitted anyway, or was not instructed to verify completion before submission.

**Producing agent:** Shorts Script Agent.

**Recommended prompt changes — two specific additions:**

One: Add to the Shorts Script Agent prompt, under the Credibility section: *"Before including any statistic, timeline, or percentage figure, flag it in brackets with the instruction [VERIFY BEFORE PUBLISH — source required]. Do not present specific figures as established fact unless you can name the study, author, or publication. If you cannot, soften the claim or remove the precision."*

Two: Add to the submission protocol: *"Before submitting a batch, confirm that every script in the numbered sequence is complete — body, visual direction, text overlay, and caption hook all present. Do not submit a batch in which any script is truncated or absent. If the full batch cannot be completed in one pass, submit completed scripts only and flag the incomplete count explicitly."*

These two changes would prevent both recurring failure types and require no architectural change to the agent's core behaviour.
