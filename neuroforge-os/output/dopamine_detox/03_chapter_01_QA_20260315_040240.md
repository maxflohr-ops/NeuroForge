### ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Unverified attribution — Kent Berridge
**Location:** "The neuroscientist Kent Berridge has spent decades separating what he calls the brain's 'wanting' system from its 'liking' system."
**Problem:** Kent Berridge is a real researcher at the University of Michigan and the wanting/liking distinction is a legitimate and well-documented framework from his lab. However, the phrasing "what he calls" implies this is Berridge's own named terminology, and no source, paper, or year is given. If a reader or fact-checker looks this up and finds even a minor discrepancy in how the concept is framed here versus in the source material, it undermines the chapter's credibility at its most important explanatory moment — the pivot on which the whole protocol argument rests. NeuroForge's credibility standard requires that named researchers and named frameworks be verifiable.
**Required fix:** The producing agent or human editor must verify this attribution against a primary source (Berridge & Robinson, 1998 is the canonical paper — "What is the role of dopamine in reward: hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience?" — but this must be confirmed, not assumed). If the framing in the chapter accurately reflects Berridge's published framework, add the year and ideally a parenthetical that anchors it: "a distinction Berridge and Robinson have documented across decades of research." If the framing is a simplification, add a qualifying phrase ("broadly consistent with Berridge's framework") so the chapter does not overstate the precision of the match.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Unsupported quantitative claim — "a hundred and fifty times a day"
**Location:** "A social media feed is a slot machine you carry in your pocket and check a hundred and fifty times a day."
**Problem:** This is a specific numeric claim presented without a source. Numbers like this circulate widely in productivity and tech-criticism writing and are frequently misattributed, outdated, or derived from a single non-representative study. If this figure is wrong, or if a reader traces it to a weak source, it damages credibility at a moment when the chapter is making a precise mechanistic argument. The brand standard requires that specific statistics be grounded.
**Required fix:** Either source this figure (identify the original study, year, and methodology and confirm it is still considered accurate) or replace it with a range that reflects what is actually documented ("studies have estimated average users unlock their phones between 80 and 150 times daily — the exact number varies, the order of magnitude does not"). Alternatively, remove the number entirely and retain the rhetorical force of the sentence without the unanchored statistic. Any of the three options is acceptable. Leaving it as is is not.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Tonal drift in the closing two paragraphs
**Location:** Final two paragraphs, beginning "Most people who run this exercise for the first time..." and "The numbers are just the calibration reading..."
**Problem:** The chapter maintains a precise, mechanism-forward register throughout — clinical where it needs to be, dry where it could be warm. The final two paragraphs soften in a way that feels consolatory rather than analytical: "This is not because they are worse than average." "The drift has been gradual enough that there was no single moment when it became obvious." These sentences are not wrong, but they shift the voice slightly toward reassurance, which is a different mode from the one the chapter has established. Kai Ren's register in this chapter earns its authority by refusing to comfort the reader — it explains, it specifies, it instructs. The closing should hold that register.
**Required fix:** Tighten the final two paragraphs to stay in the analytical register. The point that the numbers are non-judgmental is valid and should be retained — but it should be made in one lean sentence at the close of the exercise instructions, not re-stated in the final paragraphs as a soft landing. The chapter should end on the forward-facing instruction: "The next step is to find out exactly what has been driving it" is a strong closing line — everything after the exercise block that exists primarily to reassure the reader should be reduced or cut.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 4:** "Days three through fourteen" — unanchored timeframe
**Location:** "For most people dealing with calibration drift rather than clinical addiction, the primary recalibration window is days three through fourteen."
**Problem:** This is a specific and confident claim about the timeline of neurological recalibration. It is not attributed, and the precision ("days three through fourteen") implies an empirical basis that is not established. This is not the same level of risk as the Berridge attribution, but it is the kind of claim that generates reader questions ("where does that number come from?") and, if challenged publicly, requires a defensible answer. The chapter is otherwise careful to distinguish between what the mechanism does and what the protocol does — this claim blurs that line.
**Required fix:** Either source the timeframe with a reference to the research base it draws on, or soften the precision: "For most people, the most difficult period tends to fall in the first two to three days; measurable changes in threshold sensitivity typically begin to emerge across the first two weeks." This version conveys the same practical information without asserting a precision the text does not support. The editor should flag this to the faculty member for confirmation that this range reflects their reading of the literature.
**Severity:** Minor

### PASSED ELEMENTS

1. **The opening three paragraphs are exactly right.** The phone-in-hand-before-waking opening works because it describes the mechanism (conditioned motor response, prefrontal cortex offline) rather than describing a feeling. It does not say "you're probably addicted to your phone." It says: here is what happened, here is the mechanism, here is why that matters. This is the chapter earning its authority in the first 200 words.

2. **The wanting/liking distinction is used correctly and cleanly.** Despite the attribution issue flagged above, the functional deployment of the distinction — "you can want something intensely and find little satisfaction in getting it" — is accurate, precise, and directly connected to a behaviour the reader recognises. It does not oversell the science. It uses it to explain something specific.

3. **The "force vs. threshold" framing in "The Concrete Consequence" section is the strongest original contribution in the chapter.** "Discipline is a force you apply. Threshold is the level the force has to clear." This is a genuinely useful reframe that does not appear in standard productivity or dopamine-detox content. It reorients the reader's model of why they fail without blaming them and without offering false comfort. It also sets up the protocol logic in a way that will pay off in later chapters. This should be treated as a headline-level concept.

4. **The exercise protocol is specific and completable.** The three-variable measurement (physical reaches, attention switches, discomfort onset time) gives the reader an actual data point rather than a vague reflection prompt. The instruction "do not try to enjoy it" is a good example of the voice doing useful work — it heads off the most common way readers would misuse the exercise. The framing as baseline measurement rather than self-assessment is consistent with the chapter's mechanistic register.

5. **The chapter does not moralize.** At no point does it imply the reader is weak, undisciplined, or at fault. It also does not over-compensate with false reassurance. This is a difficult tonal balance to maintain across 2,000 words of content about a topic that invites both, and the chapter holds it almost entirely — the minor drift in the closing paragraphs notwithstanding.

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The Manuscript Agent produced strong voice and structure but included one unverified named attribution and one unanchored quantitative statistic. Both are in the same section (the dopamine mechanism explanation) and both appear to reflect a reliance on widely-cited secondary summaries of the science rather than primary sources. The agent did not fabricate either — Berridge is real, the statistic circulates widely — but neither was confirmed before inclusion.

**Producing agent:** Manuscript Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following instruction to the Manuscript Agent's citation handling rules:

*"When naming a specific researcher, using a specific number or statistic, or attributing a named framework to a named individual, flag it with [VERIFY] in square brackets inline. Do not remove it. Do not fabricate a source. Mark it clearly so the QA Agent and human editor know it requires confirmation before publication. A flagged claim is a solvable problem. An unflagged claim that turns out to be wrong is a brand problem."*

This creates a clear handoff protocol: the Manuscript Agent marks what it cannot confirm, the QA Agent flags anything unmarked that should have been, and the human editor has a clean list of verifications to run before sign-off. It also removes any implicit pressure on the Manuscript Agent to produce citations it cannot verify — which is likely what produced the unmarked claims in the first place.
