### ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Ungrounded measurability claim in lead magnet bullet
**Location:** Bullet Benefits, Bullet 3 — "the specific labelling language that produces a measurable neurological shift"
**Problem:** "Measurable neurological shift" implies a quantifiable, clinically verified physiological effect. The underlying research — Lieberman et al. on affect labelling — does support reduced amygdala activation, but stating this as a "measurable neurological shift" without qualification overstates certainty and edges into implicit medical claim territory. If challenged, this cannot be defended in the copy as written.
**Required fix:** Either ground the claim by naming the mechanism (e.g., "by labelling it with precision — the labelling technique associated with reduced amygdala activation in affect-labelling research") or soften the claim to "produces a documented shift in emotional intensity" and let the parenthetical explanation carry the weight. Do not remove the distinction between precise and imprecise labelling — that is a genuine and defensible insight. Only the word "measurable neurological" needs qualification or replacement.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Asserted clinical backing without attribution for thought scheduling
**Location:** Book Description — "the thought-scheduling method with clinical backing"
**Problem:** "Clinical backing" is a credibility claim. Thought scheduling (also called worry postponement) does have published clinical support — primarily from studies on GAD treatment — but stating "clinical backing" in sales copy without any grounding (a researcher name, a study type, or a qualifying phrase) makes a claim the reader cannot verify and the brand cannot defend if questioned. In the current context it functions as a trust signal borrowed without cost.
**Required fix:** Add minimal grounding: "the thought-scheduling method, drawn from clinical research on worry postponement" — or soften to "the thought-scheduling method, one of the more counterintuitive tools to emerge from clinical psychology." The second option avoids needing to specify sources while remaining honest. Either is acceptable; the current wording is not.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Unqualified clinical claim in Email 3 PS
**Location:** Email 3, postscript — "The defusion chapter also covers why cognitive restructuring — the 'is this thought really true?' technique from CBT — often fails for people with thought loops, and what to do instead."
**Problem:** This is a meaningful and defensible position in the ACT literature, but presenting it in a PS as settled fact — "often fails" — without qualification positions Nova as making a clinical judgement about a specific therapeutic technique. This is the kind of claim that, if clipped and shared out of context, could generate reputational or professional risk. The claim is not wrong. The framing is too flat.
**Required fix:** Add a brief qualifier that contextualises the claim without undermining it: "why cognitive restructuring — the 'is this thought really true?' technique from CBT — can struggle with thoughts that feel completely certain, and what defusion does instead." This preserves the insight, remains honest about the limitation, and removes the exposed edge.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 4:** "Introductory price" with no expiry logic
**Location:** Thank You Page, Book Offer — "Currently available at the introductory price of £17."
**Problem:** This is not a Red Flag, but it creates a downstream consistency problem. "Introductory price" implies the price will rise. If it does not rise, the claim becomes false urgency. If it does rise, there is no mechanism in the current funnel to update the copy. This is a production and legal hygiene issue, not a credibility crisis — but it should be resolved before launch.
**Required fix:** Either (a) remove "introductory price" and replace with "available now for £17" — clean, true, no implied scarcity; or (b) retain "introductory price" and document in the campaign brief when the price increases and who is responsible for updating the thank you page copy. Option (a) is lower risk.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 5:** Inline character-count working visible in final Google Ads copy
**Location:** Section 4, Google Ads — all three ads contain inline trimming notation (e.g., "35 chars — trim to: The Problem Isn't Willpower (29 chars)")
**Problem:** This is draft notation presented in what is labelled a "Complete Conversion Package v1.0." If passed to a designer or media buyer without further editing, it will cause errors or confusion. Final copy should show only the confirmed final version of each element with its confirmed character count.
**Required fix:** Strip all inline trimming notation. Present only the final confirmed headline and description text for each ad, with a single character count in brackets confirming it is within spec. Move the trimming working to an appendix or remove entirely.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 6:** Third-person anecdote in Email 4 produces tonal drift
**Location:** Email 4, opening three paragraphs — "She described it the same way most people do..."
**Problem:** Every other email in the sequence is written from Nova's first-person perspective, addressing the reader directly. Email 4 opens with a third-person account of an unnamed woman's experience — a journalistic framing that is functional but breaks the intimacy of the sequence. It is also the least specific piece of storytelling in the package: "She'd been through the standard repertoire" is a summary, not a scene. By contrast, Email 2's "Picture this. You're somewhere ordinary — making dinner, sitting in traffic, lying in bed" places the reader in the experience rather than observing it from outside.
**Required fix:** Consider rewriting the opening to place the reader directly in the scenario ("You know the one. It's an ordinary evening — and then that thought arrives...") rather than describing a third party. If the third-person anecdote is retained for variety, it needs one specific sensory or situational detail to earn its place. The current version is too generic to do the emotional work it is intended to do.
**Severity:** Minor

### PASSED ELEMENTS

1. **Landing page headline Option C is the strongest headline in the package.** "For people who know their thoughts aren't rational — and can't stop them anyway" names the reader at the exact moment of maximum frustration — not aspirationally, not generically. The word "anyway" does particular work: it encodes the exhaustion of the reader without editorialising about it. This is the kind of headline that makes the right reader feel seen and everyone else scroll past, which is exactly correct targeting behaviour.

"The thought is the same. Its authority is not." is the single most memorable sentence in the package — short, accurate, and reframeable. This email will generate replies.

It functions as a trust signal precisely because it does not perform urgency. Combined with the absence of countdown timers, expiry language, or artificial scarcity anywhere in the package, this creates a funnel that is consistent with the brand's credibility positioning and Nova's established voice.

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** Three of the four substantive issues in this package share the same root cause: credibility claims that gesture at scientific or clinical backing without providing minimum grounding. The copy correctly identifies real, defensible mechanisms (affect labelling, worry postponement, cognitive defusion) but in each case makes the claim slightly bolder than the visible evidence in the copy can support. The producing agent is adding authority markers ("measurable neurological," "clinical backing," "often fails") to strengthen persuasion without checking whether each marker is defensible as stated.

**Producing agent:** Funnel Agent

**Recommended prompt addition:** Add the following check to the Funnel Agent's credibility instruction block:

*"Before using any of the following terms, supply the minimum grounding that makes the claim defensible in the copy itself: 'clinically proven,' 'clinical backing,' 'research shows,' 'neurological,' 'measurable,' 'evidence-based.' Minimum grounding means: a named mechanism, a named research tradition (e.g., 'ACT research,' 'affect labelling studies'), or a qualifying phrase that correctly scopes the claim (e.g., 'in conditions of high cognitive load'). Do not use these terms as authority signals without grounding. If you cannot ground the claim in the copy, soften the claim to what you can defend."*

This single addition would have prevented Issues 1, 2, and 3 in this submission.
