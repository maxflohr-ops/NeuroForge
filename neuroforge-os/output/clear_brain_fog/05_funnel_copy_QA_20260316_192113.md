### ISSUES FOUND

---

**Issue 1:** Quantitative claim without source or hedge
**Location:** Email 2, paragraph 6 — *"blood glucose is pulled out of the bloodstream faster than the brain can buffer the drop, and for a period of fifteen to forty minutes, the brain is running short on its primary fuel"*
**Problem:** A specific time window ("fifteen to forty minutes") stated as fact. This is a precise quantitative claim about a physiological process — readers with any scientific literacy will notice the exactness and wonder where it comes from. If challenged, NeuroForge cannot point to a source. Even if the range is defensible, the unhedged framing creates credibility risk.
**Required fix:** Add a light qualifier: *"...for a period that can range from fifteen to forty minutes depending on individual insulin response..."* or remove the specific window and replace with *"...for a period long enough that most people experience it as a cognitive event rather than a physical one."* The mechanism remains intact; the liability does not.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Caffeine half-life stated as settled fact — should be hedged
**Location:** Email 3, paragraph 8 — *"with a half-life of roughly five to six hours"*
**Problem:** Caffeine half-life varies substantially by individual — genetic CYP1A2 variation means some people clear caffeine in 3 hours, others in 9 or more. Stating 5–6 hours as the reference figure without acknowledging individual variation is technically accurate as a population average but will actively mislead fast and slow metabolisers — both of whom are likely in this audience. If a fast metaboliser uses this figure to set their cutoff time and still sleeps badly, they may distrust the advice or the product.
**Required fix:** Revise to: *"with a half-life of roughly five to seven hours in most people — though individual clearance rates vary significantly based on genetics, and some people clear it considerably faster or slower than this."* The intervention advice (stop early enough for full clearance) still stands; it now holds for the full reader population.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Google Ads description lines likely exceed character limits
**Location:** Section 4, Search Ad 1, Description 1 — *"Brain fog isn't vague — it has specific biological causes. Find yours in 5 minutes with a free scored assessment."*; Search Ad 2, Description 1 — *"Sleep duration isn't the problem. Sleep architecture usually is. The free Brain Fog Diagnostic identifies the real driver."*; Search Ad 3, Description 1 — *"Coffee, willpower, and better sleep advice haven't fixed it — because they haven't identified the right mechanism. This will."*
**Problem:** Google Search Ads allow a maximum of 90 characters per description line. Description 1 in Ad 1 runs to approximately 107 characters. Ad 2 Description 1 runs to approximately 117 characters. Ad 3 Description 1 runs to approximately 118 characters. All three will be truncated or rejected at upload. The copy itself is strong — the issue is purely technical non-compliance, but it will cause production delay and potentially live errors if not caught here.
**Required fix:** Trim each description line to 90 characters maximum. Suggested targets that preserve the argument:
- Ad 1 D1: *"Brain fog has specific biological causes. Find yours in 5 minutes — free scored assessment."* (90 chars)
- Ad 2 D1: *"Sleep duration isn't the problem. Sleep architecture usually is. Find the real driver free."* (90 chars — verify)
- Ad 3 D1: *"Generic sleep advice won't fix it — you haven't identified the right mechanism yet. This will."* (94 chars — needs one more trim)
The copy team should recount all description lines against the 90-character limit before submission.
**Severity:** Major (will cause live production failure if not fixed)

**Issue 5:** Preview text in Email 4 creates a false expectation the email body does not deliver
**Location:** Email 4 — Preview text: *"She'd spent two years attributing it to burnout. It wasn't burnout."*
**Problem:** The preview text signals a specific named individual ("She") — which typically means a case study or named example is coming. The email body immediately abandons this and opens with "There's a version of this I hear often" — a composite, gender-neutral archetype rather than a named story. This is a minor trust misfire: the reader who opens based on "She'd spent two years..." and finds no "she" will register a small, subconscious disconnect. For an audience being primed to trust Dr. Hale's precision, this is worth fixing.
**Required fix:** Either (a) revise the preview text to match the composite framing the email actually uses — e.g., *"They'd spent two years attributing it to burnout. It wasn't."* — or (b) revise the email opening to deliver the specific individual the preview text promised, at least for the first two sentences before widening to the pattern.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 6:** Book description objection handler reading time calculation is too confident
**Location:** Thank You Page, Objection Handler — "On time" — *"Each chapter is 2,000–2,500 words — about fifteen minutes of reading."*
**Problem:** The implied reading speed here is approximately 160 words per minute — which is at the slower end of average. Many readers in this audience (cognitively oriented professionals — the target demographic) read at 250–300 wpm, which would make each chapter a 7–10 minute read. The claim doesn't harm these readers. But the more pressing issue: reading speed estimates in sales copy tend to read as slightly presumptuous — the reader may not know their own reading speed, may feel patronised by the specificity, or may read differently depending on content density. For a book that is explicitly mechanistic (and therefore cognitively demanding per page), 15 minutes may also be an underestimate for careful readers.
**Required fix:** Soften to a range: *"Each chapter is 2,000–2,500 words — roughly ten to fifteen minutes of reading, depending on how you engage with the mechanisms."* This is more accurate, more honest, and slightly more respectful of the reader's intelligence.
**Severity:** Minor

It names a felt experience that has no ordinary-language name, contains zero ad vocabulary, and earns the pause it creates. It should be the primary A/B test variant.

It sits in the lock without turning it."* is the most memorable sentence in the package. It is the kind of explanation that gets quoted to a partner over dinner. This is the standard every other explanatory moment in the sequence is being held to.

This is correctly executed and will convert an intelligent, sceptical audience more reliably than an aggressive offer email would.

4. **The testimonial placeholder guidance** — The specificity required from each testimonial is unusually good. "Name a specific mechanism they identified," "contrast with prior approaches," "a specific outcome within a defined timeframe" — this guidance will produce testimonials that actually convert, rather than the generic validation quotes most brands collect. The instruction that "specificity of the quotes carries more credibility than stock headshots" is correct and worth preserving.

Chapter 7's tease in particular — *"not a metaphor, but a measurable downregulation of exactly the region responsible for the clear, deliberate thinking you're trying to recover"* — is the kind of line that makes a reader trust the author before they've read a word of the book.
