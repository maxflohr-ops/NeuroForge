## ISSUES FOUND

---

**Issue 1:** Stat hook presents an unverified specific figure as established fact
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Stat or fact hooks, Hook 2: *"Research on worry consistently shows that the outcomes people ruminate on happen less than 10% of the time."*
**Problem:** This references what is likely the Borkovec et al. worry outcome research (or a downstream popularisation of it), but the figure is presented with implied precision ("less than 10%") without any attribution or qualification. If this figure is misremembered, misquoted, or from a source that doesn't stand up to scrutiny, it will enter every piece of downstream content that draws from this brief as apparent fact. This is a credibility risk, not a Red Flag — it is not fabricated, but it is unanchored. The NeuroForge standard requires that specific statistics either have a verifiable source attached in the brief or be softened to reflect the actual level of certainty.
**Required fix:** Either (a) attach the source in the brief notes so the downstream editor can verify it — e.g. "Source: Borkovec et al., 1989 — verify figure before publication" — or (b) soften the language to "Studies on worry have consistently found that the majority of feared outcomes don't occur" and remove the specific percentage until it can be verified.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Stat hook attributes a research finding to "the 1980s" without a source
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Stat or fact hooks, Hook 3: *"Thought suppression — being told to 'just stop thinking about it' — reliably increases the frequency of the thought you're trying to suppress. This was documented in the 1980s. We're still giving the wrong advice."*
**Problem:** This is almost certainly a reference to Wegner's white bear thought suppression experiments (1987). The finding is real and well-established. However, presenting it as "documented in the 1980s" without attribution creates a situation where the content sounds like it is citing a source it isn't actually citing. If an editor or reader asks "where?", there is no answer in the brief. The casual framing ("This was documented in the 1980s") has the rhetorical texture of a citation without the accountability of one. For a premium credibility-first brand, this matters — it teaches downstream agents and editors to gesture at sources rather than name them.
**Required fix:** Add a source note in the brief — e.g. "Ref: Wegner, 1987 — white bear thought suppression studies — verify before using in published content." The hook copy itself can remain as written once the source is confirmed; the issue is the missing reference in the brief, not the claim itself.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Default Mode Network hook makes a clinical assertion that requires qualification
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Stat or fact hooks, Hook 1: *"The brain's default mode network — the system responsible for self-referential thought — activates more strongly in people who ruminate, even when they're doing nothing."*
**Problem:** The core claim is consistent with published neuroscience research on rumination and the DMN. However, as written, it presents a correlational finding in the language of established fact, and the phrase "the system responsible for self-referential thought" is a simplification of the DMN's function that could be contested by anyone with neuroscience training. For a faculty member whose credibility depends on precision, this level of reduction risks undermining the brand's scientific authority with an informed audience — which, for this topic, includes clinicians and therapists. The issue is not inaccuracy; it is the confidence of the framing exceeding what the research actually supports without qualification.
**Required fix:** Add a brief qualifier in the hook — e.g. "one of the systems associated with self-referential thought" — and add a source note to the brief for the downstream editor. Alternatively, flag this hook as requiring science-check before use in any published content.
**Severity:** Minor

**Issue 4:** Angles 1 and 3 are partially redundant
**Location:** Section 5, Chapter Angle Candidates — Angle 1 (*"What overthinking actually is — and why it's not the same as thinking hard"*) and Angle 3 (*"The difference between productive problem-solving and unproductive rumination"*)
**Problem:** Both angles are built on the same core distinction — that overthinking and useful thinking are different processes. Angle 1 frames this as definitional (what is it?); Angle 3 frames it as practical (how do I tell them apart right now?). These are defensibly different, but the overlap is significant enough that a Book Architect Agent working from this brief could treat them as equivalent and collapse them, or worse, produce two chapters that feel repetitive to a reader. The brief's checklist requires that chapter angles are distinct — this pair is not clearly distinct enough without explicit guidance.
**Required fix:** Add a one-sentence differentiator to each angle's "Why it matters" note that makes the distinction explicit. For example: Angle 1 is a conceptual chapter that builds the framework; Angle 3 is a practical chapter that gives the real-time audit tool. This distinction should be stated in the brief so the Book Architect Agent treats them as occupying different jobs in the chapter sequence, not as variations on the same idea.
**Severity:** Minor

**Issue 5:** One hook has a tonal register slightly below Dr. Vale's standard
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Counterintuitive statement hooks, Hook 2: *"Your overthinking isn't making you more prepared. It's making you less capable of acting when the moment comes."*
**Problem:** This hook is functional and the logic is sound, but the register is slightly sharper and more confrontational than Dr. Vale's established voice. Her characteristic mode is to name a mechanism, not to push back at the reader. The hook reads more like a challenge ("you're wrong about this") than an explanation ("here's what's actually happening"). Every other hook in the batch handles this correctly — this one is the outlier. It would not cause brand damage if published, but it is the weakest of the fifteen and risks attributing a tone to Dr. Vale that isn't hers.
**Required fix:** Either replace this hook or soften the framing. For example: *"Overthinking feels like preparation. The research suggests it's doing the opposite."* — this preserves the counterintuitive logic while keeping the register explanatory rather than confrontational.
**Severity:** Minor

## PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The producing agent is generating confident-sounding statistical and neuroscientific claims in hook copy without attaching source notes or verification flags. The claims are not fabricated — they are consistent with real research — but they are presented without the documentation trail that a credibility-first brand requires. This is a systematic gap rather than an isolated error: three of the six stat/fact hooks in Section 7 have this issue in varying degrees.

**Producing agent:** Research Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following instruction to the Research Agent's hook-writing section:

> *"For any hook that cites a specific statistic, percentage, research finding, or named neurological mechanism, you must include a source note in brackets immediately after the hook — even if you are not certain of the exact reference. Format: [Source note: likely [researcher/study/year] — flag for verification before publication]. A hook with an unattributed specific claim is not production-ready. If you cannot attach even a probable source, reframe the hook to remove the specific claim and use qualified language instead — 'research suggests', 'studies have found', 'evidence points to' — rather than stating the finding as established fact."*

This change would prevent the same pattern from appearing in future research briefs and would create a verification trail that the human editor can work from rather than having to source-check from scratch.
