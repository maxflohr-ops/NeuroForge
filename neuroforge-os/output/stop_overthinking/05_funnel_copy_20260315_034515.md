**EMAIL BODY:**

Picture this. A conversation ended two days ago. Nothing dramatic — a slightly ambiguous tone, a reply that felt off, something left unsaid that you're not sure mattered. And since then, your brain has run the sequence maybe thirty times.

Not because you want to. Not because you're learning anything new each time. Because it won't stop.

Here's the mechanism underneath that.

The brain has a system — sometimes called the Zeigarnik effect — that flags unfinished tasks and returns to them. It's useful for remembering where you left off on a project. It's significantly less useful when the "unfinished task" is an interpersonal uncertainty that has no resolution available.

Your brain isn't replaying the conversation out of habit or weakness. It's replaying it because it has flagged something as unresolved — a perceived threat, an open question — and it keeps returning to try to close it. The problem is that what it's looking for (certainty about what the other person meant, confirmation that you said the right thing, resolution of an ambiguity that may simply not be resolvable) isn't available through more thinking.

So the loop runs again.

The practical implication of this is that the target of intervention is not the thought content. You can't think your way out of a thought-loop. The target is the sense of unresolvedeness itself — giving the brain a resolution signal that doesn't require certainty to close.

That's what the loop closure framework in the Audit is for. And it's a large part of what *Stop Overthinking* covers in detail — specifically, a decision close protocol that provides the brain with the resolution signal it's looking for, even when the underlying question hasn't fully answered itself.

**EMAIL BODY:**

Here's a thing that seems like it should help but doesn't.

You've had an interaction that's sitting uneasily. Something felt off. Your brain is running it. So you text someone who was there — or you re-read the conversation thread — or you ask the person directly: "We're fine, right?" "I didn't come across as weird?" "You're not upset with me?"

The reassurance comes. Anxiety drops. You feel — briefly — better.

Then the relief wears off.

And the anxiety comes back, usually stronger than before.

This is the reassurance loop, and it is one of the most effective maintenance mechanisms for overthinking that exists. Here's why it works the way it does.

When you seek reassurance, you get temporary relief — but what the brain has actually learned is this: *uncertainty is intolerable, and the way to manage it is to seek external resolution*. You have practised the response. The next time ambiguity arises, the pull toward reassurance is stronger, because it worked last time. The relief window gets shorter. The anxiety on the rebound gets bigger. The threshold for needing reassurance gets lower.

Most overthinkers recognise this pattern immediately when it's named. They already knew the checking wasn't really helping. What they didn't have was language for why it was actively making things worse.

The counterintuitive move — the one that actually reduces the loop over time — is to sit with the uncertainty without resolving it. Not because the discomfort is good for you in some abstract sense, but because doing so trains the nervous system that ambiguity is not a threat requiring immediate action. Every time you don't seek reassurance and the discomfort passes anyway, the loop becomes slightly less compelling the next time it fires.

This is not easy at first. But the skill is buildable.

*Stop Overthinking* has a full chapter on the reassurance trap — how to recognise all the forms it takes (not just asking others, but re-reading messages, internally arguing with the worry, checking your own behaviour for errors), and a concrete protocol for interrupting it at the moment the urge is strongest.
