## ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Potential Red Flag — Implicit Medical Mechanism Claims Presented as Diagnostic Fact
**Location:** Scripts 3, 6, 7, 8, 12, 13, 19; multiple carousel slides
**Problem:** Throughout the batch, neurological and psychological mechanisms are stated as established, certain, and universally applicable. Examples: "Your prefrontal cortex... is watching the rumination happen. But the rumination is being driven by a subcortical threat-response system that does not take instructions from your rational mind" (Script 3); "Self-criticism activates the same stress-response circuitry as external threat. Your body cannot distinguish between..." (Script 7); "Insufficient sleep elevates amygdala reactivity and reduces prefrontal regulation" (Script 20). These claims are directionally consistent with existing research but are stated with a level of certainty that the research does not fully support — and they are stated about the viewer's brain specifically, not as general tendencies. The cumulative effect is a series of implicit diagnostic statements: "your brain does X," "your nervous system cannot do Y." This sits at the edge of the medical claim Red Flag. It does not cross the line into diagnosing or treating a condition, but the pattern is close enough to require a mitigation strategy across the batch.
**Required fix:** Add qualifying language to mechanism claims throughout. "Research suggests," "in people who ruminate frequently," "one of the mechanisms thought to be involved" — these do not weaken the authority of the content; they are accurate. A single editorial pass with the instruction "qualify all mechanism claims as tendencies or research-supported patterns rather than universal facts about the viewer's brain" would resolve this across the batch. This is not a rewrite — it is a language calibration.
**Severity:** Critical

**Issue 2:** Word Count Violations — Scripts 3, 12, and 20 Exceed 150-Word Body Limit for 60-Second Format
**Location:** Script 3 (estimated 220 words in body); Script 12 (estimated 210 words in body); Script 20 (estimated 230 words in body)
**Problem:** The short-form checklist requires body copy under 150 words for a 60-second script. Scripts 3, 12, and 20 are each estimated at 45–50 seconds duration, but their body word counts significantly exceed this threshold. At natural speaking pace for Dr. Nova Vale's measured delivery style (approximately 120–130 words per minute), these scripts run 90–110 seconds — nearly double the stated duration. This is not a formatting issue. It is a production problem: if these are cut at the platform's time limit, the scripts will end mid-thought.
**Required fix:** Each of the three scripts requires a structural cut, not a trim. The producing agent should be instructed to identify the single central mechanism in each script and remove the secondary elaboration. Script 3's suppression research paragraph is a candidate for removal — the main point lands without it. Script 12's "partially true and mostly false" elaboration can be compressed. Script 20's cognitive load section is the least essential of the three variables and is the most underdeveloped — cut or compress.
**Severity:** Critical

**Issue 3:** "Research on thought suppression shows it reliably increases the frequency of the thought you're trying to suppress" — Unqualified Citation
**Location:** Script 3, paragraph 5: "Research on thought suppression shows it reliably increases the frequency of the thought you're trying to suppress."
**Problem:** This is a reference to a real body of research (Wegner's white bear studies and subsequent replication work), but it is stated as if citing a single, settled finding when the literature on ironic rebound is more nuanced — effects vary by individual, suppression strategy, and context. More importantly, it is stated without any attribution or caveat. "Research shows" with no further qualification reads as a fabricated or vague citation in the absence of a named source or research tradition. This does not trigger the fabricated citation Red Flag because the underlying research exists, but the framing is imprecise in a way that risks credibility.
**Required fix:** Either name the research tradition ("studies on thought suppression — most famously the 'white bear' experiments — suggest...") or qualify the claim ("evidence suggests that suppression often increases..."). Do not remove the claim — it is accurate and useful. Qualify it.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 5:** Script 3 — Dual-Idea Structure
**Location:** Script 3, full body
**Problem:** Script 3 carries two distinct ideas: (1) why awareness of overthinking doesn't stop it (dual-brain-system explanation), and (2) why thought suppression makes things worse, followed by an alternative (noticing without engaging). These are related but they are not the same idea, and the script attempts to deliver both in a 50-second format. The checklist requires one idea per script. The suppression section begins "The instruction 'just stop thinking about it' is one of the least effective..." — this is a complete second mechanism that could anchor its own script.
**Required fix:** Split Script 3 into two scripts. Script 3A: why awareness doesn't stop overthinking (the dual-system explanation). Script 3B: why suppression fails and what noticing without engaging actually means. This also resolves part of the word count issue flagged in Issue 2.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 6:** Script 5 — Closing Line Voice Risk
**Location:** Script 5, second-to-last paragraph: "You're not too much. Your nervous system learned something. And learned things can be updated."
**Problem:** "You're not too much" is a phrase with a strong association with therapeutic affirmation culture and social media self-help content. **Required fix:** Remove "You're not too much" and replace with a line that maintains the clinical register. Instruction to producing agent: the reframe should name the trait accurately, not offer reassurance. The existing line about the trait vs. the pattern is strong — the closing should reinforce that distinction, not pivot to affirmation.

## PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** Credibility qualification — mechanism claims stated as universal facts about the viewer's brain rather than research-supported tendencies. This appeared across 7 of 20 scripts and in multiple carousel slides. It is a consistent pattern, not an isolated error.

**Producing agent:** Manuscript/Shorts Script Agent

**Recommended prompt change:** Add the following constraint to the Shorts Script Agent prompt under a "Credibility" or "Mechanism Claims" section:

> *When referencing neurological or psychological mechanisms (brain regions, nervous system responses, research findings), qualify claims as tendencies rather than universal facts. Use language such as "research suggests," "in people who ruminate frequently," "one mechanism thought to be involved," or "evidence from [field/tradition] indicates." Do not state mechanism claims in the form "your brain does X" or "your body cannot do Y" — these read as diagnostic statements and sit at the edge of the medical claim prohibition. The authority of the content does not depend on certainty; it depends on precision.*

This single addition would catch the majority of the credibility pattern seen in this batch without constraining the voice or the quality of the mechanism explanations — which are otherwise strong.
