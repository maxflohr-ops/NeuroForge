You're in a difficult conversation, or you've sent an email you're not entirely sure about, or you've made a decision that felt fine at the time. Something unresolved is sitting in the background of your mind — not urgent, just present.

You mention it to someone you trust. They tell you it's fine. That you're reading too much into it, that the decision was reasonable, that you couldn't have known. And for a short time — maybe twenty minutes, maybe an hour — the loop quiets.

Then it comes back. Slightly louder than before.

If this is familiar, I want to tell you precisely what is happening, because understanding it is the first real interruption.

When the brain encounters unresolved uncertainty, it registers it as a potential threat. The amygdala does not wait for evidence before activating — it responds to the possibility of a problem, not the confirmation of one. And the loop is what happens when it keeps trying to resolve that uncertainty and cannot find a resolution that satisfies the threshold for safety.

Reassurance provides that resolution — briefly. Someone you trust signals that the threat is not real. The amygdala settles. The loop quiets.

But the underlying uncertainty hasn't changed. Nothing has been resolved — it has been covered over. And the moment the reassurance fades, the original signal is still there, unanswered. The brain returns to it. This time, the bar is higher, because the previous resolution didn't hold.

Over time — and this is the part that matters most — the brain learns that reassurance is the exit ramp. Its tolerance for sitting with uncertainty decreases. It becomes more dependent on the external signal. The loops become more frequent, and the relief they provide becomes shorter.

This is the reassurance trap. And it operates in three forms, not one: asking other people, searching for information online, and the internal version — telling yourself repeatedly that it will probably be fine.

All three follow the same architecture. All three provide relief. All three make the next loop more likely.

The move out of this is not to suppress the urge to seek reassurance. It is to understand what the urge is telling you — that the brain has registered unresolved uncertainty as a threat — and to address that directly, at the level of the uncertainty, rather than covering it over.

**Email body:**

There's an instruction I give people that sounds, on first hearing, like exactly the wrong thing to do.

*Stop trying to make the thought go away.*

Not because the thought doesn't matter. Not because you should accept everything your mind produces without question. But because the act of trying to eliminate a thought is, neurologically, a form of engagement with it — and engagement is what keeps it active.

You may already know the classic experiment: try not to think about a white bear for the next sixty seconds. Most people find that the suppression attempt produces the opposite of the intended result. The thought doesn't disappear — it intensifies, because the brain has to hold the target in working memory in order to monitor for its absence. The act of not-thinking-about-something requires actively thinking about it.

The same architecture applies to anxious thoughts. When we try to push them away, argue them out of existence, or distract ourselves from them, we are engaging with them — treating them as problems that require a response. That engagement confirms their status as things worth attending to. The loop runs.

What actually works is something different. Not suppression, and not engagement. Something closer to observation.

The psychological term is cognitive defusion — a technique from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy that changes your relationship to a thought without requiring you to change the thought itself. Instead of treating the thought as a fact to be verified or disproved, you treat it as mental activity to be observed.

The simplest form of this sounds like a small shift, but the effect is significant:

Instead of: *"Something could go terribly wrong."*
Try: *"I notice I'm having the thought that something could go terribly wrong."*

That's it. One layer of distance between you and the content. The thought doesn't disappear — but you are no longer inside it. You are watching it pass.

This is not a trick. It reflects something real about how the brain processes its own activity. When you label a mental event as a mental event rather than a report about reality, the amygdala's response to it is measurably different. The urgency drops. The loop loses some of its pull.

The reason most people haven't tried this — or have tried it and found it ineffective — is that it requires practice in a non-activated state before it's available in an activated one. When you're in the middle of a loop, you don't have the cognitive bandwidth to learn something new. You can only access what's already been encoded.

So practise it today, with something small. A minor worry. A low-stakes second thought. Run it through the defusion language and notice what changes in the felt quality of the thought.

That felt difference is the thing I want you to have a reference point for.
