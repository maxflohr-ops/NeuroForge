### ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Unverified and potentially fabricated statistic
**Location:** Hook 4 — *"The average person makes over 35,000 decisions a day."*
**Problem:** This figure is widely circulated in self-help content but has no credible scientific source. The study most often cited as its origin cannot be verified, and the number itself is contested. NeuroForge's credibility standard explicitly prohibits claims that *may* be invented — and a number this specific, this frequently misused, qualifies. If a journalist or skeptical reader investigates this and finds no credible source, it damages the brand. A fabricated-feeling statistic also undercuts the deliberate, no-nonsense tone of the Marcus Voss voice.
**Required fix:** Either source this to a specific, verifiable study and include the citation in the brief, or replace Hook 4 with a hook that makes the same point without a specific number (e.g., *"Most of your decisions today were already made for you — by habit, default, and whoever you felt like being when you woke up."*). Do not publish with the number unverified.
**Severity:** Critical

---

**Issue 2:** Unanchored specific claim — the "30-second" resistance moment
**Location:** Hook 11 — *"Most people don't know there's a single moment where every discipline break actually happens — and it's not when you quit. It's about 30 seconds earlier."*
**Problem:** The hook is conceptually excellent — one of the strongest in the batch — but the phrase *"about 30 seconds earlier"* presents as a research finding rather than a rhetorical device. If a reader asks "where does that figure come from?", there is no answer. The specific number is doing credibility work it hasn't earned. The *idea* behind the hook (that there is a predictable pre-decision moment) is consistent with research on temptation and impulse, but "30 seconds" is not a finding — it is a figure that has been invented for rhetorical effect, and Marcus Voss's voice requires that those two things don't blur together.
**Required fix:** Remove the specific time reference. Revise to something like: *"Most people don't know there's a single moment where every discipline break actually happens — and it's not when you quit. It's a few seconds before, when you're still pretending you haven't already decided."* The hook retains its punch without the unverifiable precision.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 3:** Search phrases in Navigational Intent section are not actual search phrases
**Location:** Section 4, Navigational Intent — search phrases listed include *"Jocko Willink discipline system"*, *"David Goggins self discipline"*, *"Atomic Habits for discipline"*, *"stoicism and self discipline"*, *"identity based habits James Clear"*
**Problem:** The QA checklist requires that search intent framing includes *actual search phrases, not paraphrases*. These read like a human's sensible estimate of what people search, which may or may not match real search volume and intent. *"Identity based habits James Clear"* in particular is almost certainly not how people phrase this query. The system prompt flags this explicitly: paraphrases are not sufficient. If these are used to inform keyword strategy or content targeting without verification, they may optimise for phrases that nobody actually searches.
**Required fix:** Pull verified search data (from Ahrefs, SEMrush, Google Search Console, or Google's autocomplete at minimum) and replace these with confirmed search phrases. If the intent framing is conceptual rather than operational, flag it explicitly as directional and note that phrase verification is required before this is used for SEO or paid acquisition.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 4:** One desired outcome drifts toward sentiment
**Location:** Section 3, Desired Outcome 7 — *"To become the kind of man whose behaviour earns his own respect."*
**Problem:** This is the weakest of the seven outcomes and the one most likely to appear in generic men's self-help content. It is not wrong, but it is looser than the six that precede it — *"earn his own respect"* is an emotional framing rather than a behavioural one, and it sits in mild tension with Marcus Voss's structural, mechanical voice. The previous six outcomes are behavioural and specific. This one is aspirational and could be lifted from any men's lifestyle brand.
**Required fix:** Either cut this outcome (the seven are not all needed) or sharpen it to a behavioural framing. Example direction: *"To stop tracking the gap between intention and behaviour because the gap has been closed — not optimistically, but factually."* The revision is not required, only the principle: the outcome should describe a state, not invoke a feeling.
**Severity:** Minor

---

**Issue 5:** Hook 6 makes a research claim without a source
**Location:** Hook 6 — *"Research on habit failure shows most people quit within the first two weeks — not because the goal was wrong, but because the architecture behind it was never built."*
**Problem:** The phrase *"Research on habit failure shows..."* invokes the authority of research without naming any research. This is a lower-risk version of Issue 1 — the claim is directionally plausible (there is genuine research on habit attrition, including work associated with Phillippa Lally's habit formation studies) — but the phrasing borrows credibility it hasn't cited. NeuroForge's standard is full credibility, which means either cite a real study or don't invoke "research" as a source.
**Required fix:** Either name the research (*"A 2010 UCL study on habit formation found..."* if the figure can be tied to Lally's data) or remove the research attribution and make the claim from observation: *"Most people quit a new habit within the first two weeks — not because the goal was wrong, but because the architecture behind it was never built."* The claim is strong enough to stand without the borrowed authority.
**Severity:** Minor

### PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The Research Agent produced two unanchored specific numbers (35,000 decisions, 30-second resistance moment) and one unverified research attribution ("research on habit failure shows"). In each case, the number is doing rhetorical work — it makes the hook more precise and therefore more credible-sounding — but the precision is fictional rather than sourced. This is a recurring pattern in AI-generated hooks and briefs: specific numbers increase perceived authority, so the system generates them even when they cannot be verified.

**Producing agent:** Research Agent

**Recommended prompt addition:** Add the following constraint to the Research Agent's hook generation instructions:

*"When writing hooks that include specific numbers, statistics, or research attributions, you must flag each one with [VERIFY] inline — e.g., 'The average person makes over 35,000 decisions a day [VERIFY].' Do not present invented or unverified specifics as established facts. If a hook works better with a specific number but you cannot verify the number, write the hook without the number and note in brackets: [A specific stat here would strengthen this hook — requires verified source before use.] Precision that cannot be sourced is a credibility liability, not an asset."*

This change would have caught all three credibility issues in this brief at the generation stage rather than the QA stage.
