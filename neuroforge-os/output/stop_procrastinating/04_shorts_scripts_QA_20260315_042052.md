## ISSUES FOUND

---

**Issue 1:** Overclaimed neuroscience — brain scan / neural regions claim
**Location:** Script 9, paragraph 3: *"Research on temporal discounting shows that when people are shown brain scans, the neural regions that activate when thinking about your future self are the same regions that activate when thinking about a stranger — not yourself."*
**Problem:** This is a real finding (associated with Ersner-Hershfield et al., 2009), but the phrasing "Research on temporal discounting shows that when people are shown brain scans" is both imprecise and reads like a fabricated or embellished citation. It does not name the study, the researchers, or the methodology, but it speaks with the authority of a specific study. If the citation is correct, it should be attributable. If it cannot be verified, it cannot be stated this way. NeuroForge's credibility standard requires that no claim be dressed as cited research unless it can be verified and attributed. This is the closest the batch comes to a Red Flag.
**Required fix:** Either (a) verify the specific study and attribute it — "In a 2009 study by Hershfield and colleagues..." — or (b) strip the research framing entirely and restate as a conceptual point: "Your brain does not treat your future self the way it treats your present self. That's not a metaphor — it's a structural feature of how identity and time interact in the brain." Do not leave the current phrasing intact.
**Severity:** Critical

---

**Issue 2:** Overclaimed mechanistic certainty — prefrontal cortex claim
**Location:** Script 17, paragraph 5: *"This works because a countdown activates the prefrontal cortex — your executive function — and interrupts the default pull toward avoidance before it solidifies into a decision."*
**Problem:** The five-second countdown technique exists (associated with Mel Robbins), but the specific neurological mechanism described here — that counting backwards activates the prefrontal cortex in a way that interrupts avoidance — is stated as established fact with no basis that can be verified. This is a plausible-sounding mechanistic explanation that may be invented or substantially oversimplified. Publishing this as fact exposes the brand to credibility damage if a knowledgeable reader challenges it.
**Required fix:** Remove the causal neurological claim. Replace with a behavioural framing: "This works because the countdown creates a decision point — a concrete moment of commitment that substitutes for the internal negotiation your brain would otherwise run." This is accurate, verifiable, and still sounds like Kai Ren. Do not retain the prefrontal cortex framing without a citable source.
**Severity:** Critical

---

**Issue 3:** Overclaimed mechanism — "threat signalling" as brain science
**Location:** Script 12, paragraph 2: *"your brain registers that internal pressure as threat signalling. The part of your brain responsible for threat detection doesn't know the difference between external danger and the self-generated pressure of trying to override avoidance. It responds the same way — by increasing resistance."*
**Problem:** This is a plausible popularisation of stress-response research, but "the part of your brain responsible for threat detection doesn't know the difference between external danger and self-generated pressure" is stated as neurological fact. It conflates amygdala threat response with performance anxiety in a way that is not precisely accurate. The claim that forcing effort increases resistance is well-grounded behaviourally — the neurological explanation as stated is not. If challenged, it would not hold.
**Required fix:** Replace the neurological mechanism with a behavioural description: "When you grit your teeth and try to force a start, you are adding a second aversive experience on top of the first one — the discomfort of the task, plus the discomfort of the internal struggle. That compounding effect makes the task feel more costly, not less." This is accurate, still counterintuitive, and doesn't require a neurological claim it can't back.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 4:** Overclaimed dopamine mechanism
**Location:** Script 6, paragraph 2: *"Your brain releases dopamine not as a reward for completing tasks, but in anticipation of reward."*
**Problem:** This is a widely circulated simplification of dopamine research (anticipatory vs. consummatory reward), but it is stated as a settled, simple fact when the neuroscience is substantially more complex and contested. The broader point — that variable, fast rewards compete with delayed-return work — is valid and well-supported. But presenting a reductive dopamine model as established science is the kind of overclaim that erodes brand credibility with informed readers, and NeuroForge's audience likely includes some.
**Required fix:** Soften the certainty: "Dopamine doesn't function purely as a reward signal — much of the dopamine response happens in anticipation of reward, not at completion. Fast, variable stimuli generate that anticipatory signal far more reliably than deep work does." This preserves the insight without overclaiming the neuroscience.
**Severity:** Major

**Issue 7:** The "open loops" claim attributed to "psychologists" without attribution
**Location:** Script 10, paragraph 2: *"Every unfinished, deferred, or avoided task stays open in what psychologists call an open loop — an active background process your brain is running in parallel with whatever you're trying to focus on."*
**Problem:** The "open loop" / Zeigarnik effect framing is legitimate and well-documented. However, the phrase "what psychologists call" implies formal clinical or academic usage without naming the concept or its origin. This is a lesser credibility issue than Issues 1–4, but it is sloppy — it gestures at authority without providing it.
**Required fix:** Either name the concept — "what's sometimes called the Zeigarnik effect — the brain's tendency to keep unfinished tasks active in working memory" — or strip the attribution and own the claim directly in Kai Ren's voice: "Every unfinished task stays live in your brain as a background process."
**Severity:** Minor

## PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure:** The primary recurring failure in this batch is neuroscience overclaiming — stating mechanistic explanations (dopamine, prefrontal cortex, threat detection) as established facts rather than as conceptual framings. This occurred in at least four scripts (6, 9, 12, 17) and represents a systematic tendency rather than isolated errors. The secondary failure is word count drift — the agent consistently wrote to idea completeness rather than to time constraint, producing scripts that exceed 150 words of spoken content across approximately five of the twenty scripts.

**Producing agent:** Shorts Script Agent

**Recommended prompt additions:**

Add the following to the Shorts Script Agent prompt:

> **On neuroscience claims:** You may reference psychological and neurological concepts, but you may never state a mechanistic explanation as established fact unless it is directly attributable to a named, verifiable study. If you cannot name the study, you must frame the claim behaviourally, not neurologically. Permitted: "Fast rewards train your brain to deprioritise slow-return tasks." Not permitted: "Your brain releases dopamine in anticipation of reward, not at completion" — unless sourced. When in doubt, frame behaviourally.

> **On word count:** Before finalising any script, count the spoken words in the hook + body + reframe + action step combined. This total must not exceed 150 words for scripts estimated at 60 seconds or under, and 180 words for scripts estimated at 90 seconds. If it exceeds the limit, cut from the reframe or action step first — the mechanism explanation is the core and should be preserved. Submit the word count alongside each script.
