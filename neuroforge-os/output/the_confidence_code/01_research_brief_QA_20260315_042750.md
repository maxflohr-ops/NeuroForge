## ISSUES FOUND

**Issue 1:** Hook 4 uses research attribution without a source
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Stat or fact hooks, Hook 4: *"Research on attachment consistently shows that how safe we felt expressing ourselves as children becomes the template for how safe we feel expressing ourselves as adults."*
**Problem:** The phrase "research on attachment consistently shows" is attribution-adjacent language. It implies a body of evidence is being accurately reported, which invites readers and editors to look for that evidence. The claim itself is a reasonable characterisation of attachment theory, but "consistently shows" is stronger than the brief can support without a cited body of literature. If this language migrates into published content unreferenced, it reads as a soft fabrication — invoking scientific authority without accountability. This is a credibility risk.
**Required fix:** Soften the attribution language to reflect a theoretical framework rather than a research consensus. Change to something like: *"Attachment theory proposes that how safe we felt expressing ourselves as children becomes the template for how safe we feel expressing ourselves as adults."* Alternatively, if the Research Agent can provide a genuine citation (Bowlby, Ainsworth, or a specific contemporary study), add it. Do not leave the phrase "research consistently shows" unsupported.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 2:** Hook 5 presents the fawn response as established neuroscience without qualification
**Location:** Section 7, Hook Options — Stat or fact hooks, Hook 5: *"The fawn response — freezing, appeasing, and shrinking under social pressure — is as automatic as fight or flight. Most people have never been taught to recognise it in themselves."*
**Problem:** Two issues compound here. First, the fawn response is a clinical concept popularised by Pete Walker in the context of complex PTSD — it is not a term that appears in mainstream neuroscience literature with the same evidential weight as fight or flight, which has decades of peer-reviewed physiological research behind it. Equating the two as equally established ("as automatic as fight or flight") overstates the scientific grounding of the fawn framing. Second, the definition given — "freezing, appeasing, and shrinking" — conflates fawn with freeze, which are distinct responses in the original framework. This is a technical inaccuracy that an informed reader or reviewer will catch, and which damages credibility if published as authoritative. This is not a Red Flag–level fabrication, but it is a meaningful overclaim.
**Required fix:** Either (a) attribute the fawn concept to Pete Walker explicitly and soften the comparative claim ("a pattern Pete Walker described as the fawn response — the tendency to appease and shrink under social pressure — operates as automatically as better-known stress responses, but most people have never been taught to name it"), or (b) remove the fight-or-flight comparison entirely and let the hook stand on its own accuracy. Separate fawn from freeze in the description.
**Severity:** Major

---

**Issue 3:** Chapter Angles 10 and 4 are partially overlapping
**Location:** Section 5, Chapter Angle Candidates — Angle 4 (*People-pleasing as a learned survival strategy, not a character flaw*) and Angle 10 (*Setting relational limits as an act of care, not rejection*)
**Problem:** Both angles are structured as reframes that remove shame and create permission for change. Angle 4 reframes people-pleasing; Angle 10 reframes limit-setting. The underlying mechanism is identical — cognitive reframe of a behaviour the reader feels guilty about. If both became chapters in the book, they would require significant differentiation at the execution stage to avoid feeling like the same chapter wearing different clothes. The checklist requirement is that every chapter angle is distinct — this pair does not fully pass that bar.
**Required fix:** The angles can remain as candidates but the overlap should be flagged for the Book Architect Agent. One option: sharpen Angle 10 away from the reframe structure entirely and toward the practical mechanics of how limits are communicated and held — making it an execution chapter rather than a permission chapter. The reframe work can live in Angle 4.
**Severity:** Minor

**Issue 4:** One search phrase is a paraphrase, not an actual search query
**Location:** Section 4, Search Intent Framing — Transformational intent search phrases: *"how to set limits without feeling guilty"*
**Problem:** Real users do not search for "limits" in this context — they search for "boundaries." This is not a trivial distinction. Search phrase accuracy affects SEO planning and content titling downstream. A paraphrase in this position — likely introduced to avoid using the word "boundaries" prematurely — produces incorrect data for the content team.
**Required fix:** Replace *"how to set limits without feeling guilty"* with *"how to set boundaries without feeling guilty"* — the actual search phrase. If the word "boundaries" is intentionally avoided in Luna Hart's voice, note that separately; it should not affect the accuracy of the search phrase documentation.
**Severity:** Minor

## PROMPT IMPROVEMENT NOTE

**Pattern of failure observed:** The Research Agent produced two hooks that use scientific or clinical framing without the precision required to support it. Hook 4 invokes research consensus without a source; Hook 5 equates a clinical concept (fawn response) with an established physiological mechanism (fight or flight) without attribution or qualification. Neither is fabricated outright, but both would read as overclaiming to an informed audience.

**Producing agent:** Research Agent

**Recommended prompt addition:** Add the following instruction to the Research Agent's hook-writing guidelines:

*"When hooks reference research, neuroscience, psychology, or clinical concepts, apply the following rules: (1) If invoking a body of research, either name the field/researcher specifically or soften the language to 'research suggests' rather than 'research shows' or 'research consistently shows.' (2) If invoking a clinical concept (fawn response, attachment styles, cognitive distortions, etc.), name the framework or theorist it comes from — do not treat it as general scientific consensus unless it is. (3) Do not compare a framework-derived concept to an established physiological mechanism unless the comparison is accurate and defensible."*

This addition would catch both failures in this brief at the generation stage and reduce credibility risk across all faculty briefs where clinical psychology terminology appears.
