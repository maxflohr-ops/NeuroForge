# Chapter 1: The Person You Become Around Other People

There is a version of you that exists only in certain rooms.

You know the one. The version that edits the punchline before it leaves your mouth, decides the opinion isn't worth saying, laughs at something you didn't find funny, agrees to something you haven't checked yet. The version that appears when a specific person walks in, or when the dynamic in the room shifts, or when someone asks a question in a tone that makes you want to answer carefully. You might not be able to name exactly when the switch happens — it's fast, and it happens before you've decided anything. But you know the feeling that arrives in its wake: a kind of flatness, a low background hum of *that wasn't quite right*, the sensation of having been present without having quite shown up.

This is not a character flaw. It is a pattern — specific, learnable, and changeable. But first, it needs a name. You cannot work with something you can only feel.

---

## Confidence Is Not What Most Books Say It Is

The conventional version of confidence is a performance story. Stand up straighter. Speak first. Project certainty. Take up more space. The assumption underneath all of this advice is that confidence is about how you appear — and that if you can manage the appearance convincingly enough, the internal experience will eventually follow.

For some people, in some situations, that works. If you are anxious about a presentation and you slow your breathing and plant your feet and lower your voice, your nervous system will often settle. The body does influence the mind. That part is real.

But it is not the problem you are here with.

Your problem is not how you come across to a room of strangers. Your problem is who you become around specific people who matter to you. It is the way your confidence doesn't just wobble in those moments — it leaves. The version of you that knows what you think, knows what you want, can hold a position and speak without editing — that version recedes. And no amount of standing up straighter in the mirror has helped with that, because the mirror is not where it happens.

What you are dealing with is not a confidence problem in the general sense. It is a *relational* confidence problem — and that distinction changes everything about how to address it.

Relational confidence is the specific form of self-trust that operates when another person has access to you. It is the internal stability that lets you stay in your own perception of a situation when someone else has a different one. It is the quality that allows you to say what you actually think without calculating in advance whether the room will receive it warmly. It is the ability to feel, in the presence of someone else's discomfort or disapproval or disappointment, that you are still okay.

This kind of confidence doesn't live in posture or vocal delivery. It lives in the dynamic between you and the people who matter — and when it collapses, it collapses there, too.

---

## Where Confidence Actually Goes

One of the most disorienting things about this pattern is how situational it is. On your own, you are often completely fine. Competent. Clear-headed. You know what you think about things. You have opinions, preferences, standards. You can make a decision without agonising over it. You might even feel confident.

Then you walk into a specific situation — a conversation with someone whose approval matters to you, a meeting where a particular person is present, a relationship where you have learned, without anyone ever saying it, to manage the atmosphere carefully — and something shifts. The clarity that was available five minutes ago becomes less available. You start monitoring. You start adjusting. The question of what you actually think gets quietly replaced by the question of what the other person is thinking.

This is not weakness. It is a learned response to a specific relational environment, and it makes complete sense when you understand where it came from. But before we go there — and we will, in the next chapter — it helps to get very clear on *where* and *when* it shows up for you. Because the pattern will not be the same for everyone reading this. It activates in particular conditions, with particular people, around particular dynamics. Yours is specific to you.

That is exactly what makes it workable.

---

## Five Patterns, One Architecture

Over the years of working with people on relational confidence, I've found that the experiences that bring them to this work tend to cluster into five recognisable patterns. They share an underlying architecture — the same roots, the same mechanism — but they show up differently in behaviour, and they require slightly different tools.

Most readers will recognise themselves in more than one. Many will have a primary pattern and secondary ones that orbit it.

**Approval-seeking** is the pattern of requiring external confirmation before you can feel okay. You scan conversations for signs that the other person is pleased, reassured, warm toward you. When you can't find those signs — or worse, when you detect irritation or withdrawal — the floor shifts. The scan doesn't feel optional. It feels like gathering necessary information about whether you are safe.

**Boundary collapse** is the pattern of setting a limit in a relationship and then taking it back, usually within minutes or hours of the other person expressing displeasure. You might tell yourself you are being flexible, reconsidering, not wanting to be rigid. But underneath, what has happened is that you could not tolerate their disappointment. The limit mattered. You abandoned it anyway.

**Over-apologising** is the pattern of framing your presence — your needs, your feelings, your opinions, your existence in the interaction — as a potential inconvenience requiring pre-emptive justification. "Sorry, I just want to say..." "Sorry, this is probably a stupid question..." "Sorry for going on about this." Each apology is small. The cumulative effect is significant.

**Social shrinking** is the pattern of making yourself less visible, less opinionated, less present in certain social situations or with certain people. You speak more quietly. You take up less physical space. You edit the joke before it arrives. You say less than you meant to. You leave conversations feeling like a flattened version of yourself and are often unable to explain exactly what happened.

**Validation dependency** is the pattern of being unable to act on a decision, hold a position, or believe a good thing about yourself until someone else has confirmed it. Your own read of a situation doesn't feel fully real until an outside source endorses it. You know what you think — but you don't quite trust it yet.

These five patterns are not character types. They are learned strategies, and they share a single underlying function: they manage the threat of social disapproval and relational loss. They worked, in some context. They cost you now.

## The Confidence Trigger

Within each of these patterns, there is usually a specific social cue that switches them on. I call this a *confidence trigger* — not in the pop-psychology sense of a source of distress, but as a precise descriptor: a specific input that activates the pattern.

For some people, the trigger is a particular tone of voice. Someone sounding slightly clipped, or flat, or tired, and suddenly you are recalibrating the entire interaction around what you might have done wrong. For others, it is a specific type of person — authority figures, people who remind them of a parent, someone whose regard feels particularly high-stakes. For others still, it is a relational dynamic: being in a group where the social hierarchy is unclear, or being in a one-on-one conversation with someone you are trying to impress, or navigating a conflict where the other person becomes emotionally elevated.

Confidence triggers are not random, even when they feel like they are. They are clues. They point back toward the original learning that installed the pattern — and they point forward toward the specific situations where you will need to use the tools in this book.

For now, you don't need to fully understand your triggers. You just need to start noticing them.

## Why the Standard Advice Doesn't Work Here

Most confidence advice addresses the performance layer. It gives you things to say, ways to stand, scripts for situations. Some of it is genuinely useful for performance anxiety. Almost none of it works for relational confidence problems, and I want to be direct about why.

Affirmations do not work because the problem is not a negative thought you are having — it is an automatic nervous system response that fires before you have had any thoughts at all. By the time you are aware of the situation, the pattern is already running.

Power poses do not work because the problem is not physical self-presentation — it is the internal shift that happens when you are in proximity to someone whose approval matters to you, and no posture you adopt beforehand will stabilise something that destabilises in that specific relational field.

"Fake it till you make it" does not work because you are not performing for strangers — you are trying to be real with people you are close to, and the gap between the performed version and the actual one is exactly the source of your exhaustion.

None of these approaches fail because the people offering them are wrong in general. They fail because they are addressing a different problem. You are dealing with a pattern that formed in relationship, which means it has to be addressed in relationship. That is what this book is built to do.

## The Confidence Map

The first tool is a structured reflection — not therapy, not journaling, but a working map. You are going to use it as your reference throughout this entire book, because every exercise that follows will be more useful when it is anchored to your specific situations rather than to abstract hypotheticals.

Identify three situations or relationships where you recognise the edited, smaller, more careful version of yourself appearing. They do not need to be dramatic. The most important ones are often ordinary — a weekly meeting, a conversation with a parent, a friendship where you always go along.

For each situation, answer these five prompts in writing:

**1. Who is present?**
Name the person or describe the dynamic. Be specific. "My manager" or "my partner when they're frustrated" or "a group where I feel like I don't know the hierarchy yet."

**2. What do I edit or withhold?**
What do you not say, not ask, not show? What version of yourself do you leave at the door? This might be opinions, feelings, humour, needs, disagreement, or simply the parts of you that feel like too much for this particular room.

**3. What do I fear will happen if I don't?**
Follow this one honestly. "They'll be annoyed." "They'll think less of me." "It will create an atmosphere." "They'll leave." This is where the pattern's logic lives.

**4. What do I feel in my body in that moment?**
Chest tightening, voice going quieter than usual, a kind of blankness, sudden fatigue, the urge to agree. You do not need to have the vocabulary for it yet. Describe it as simply as you can.

**5. What does the smaller version of me look like to others?**
From the outside. How would someone who didn't know you describe this version of you? Quieter than expected. Very agreeable. Hard to read. Difficult to get a strong opinion out of. This is not self-criticism — it is useful information.

Keep this map. Write it by hand if you can, and keep it somewhere you can return to. Every chapter in this book will ask you to bring a real situation to the exercise. The situations on this map are yours.

---

There is something clarifying about the moment you stop experiencing this as a general deficiency — *I am just not a confident person* — and start seeing it as a specific, situated pattern with identifiable features. It doesn't make it smaller. But it makes it workable. The person you become around other people was shaped by something, which means the shape can change. To understand how that shaping happened, we need to go back a little further — to the original relationships where you first learned whether it was safe to be fully yourself.
